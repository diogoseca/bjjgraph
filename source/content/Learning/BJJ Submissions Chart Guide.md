# BJJ Submissions Chart: Complete Guide to Finishing Techniques

**BJJ submissions** are the finishing techniques that end matches via tap-out, applying mechanical force to joints or restricting blood/air flow to the brain. This guide maps 30+ submissions by position, success rate, and belt level — giving you a strategic framework to build a complete finishing game from white belt to black belt.

**Quick reference:** The highest-percentage submission is the [[Rear Naked Choke]] (~80% success at advanced level from [[Back Control]]). For beginners, start with: Rear Naked Choke → [[Americana]] → [[Triangle Choke]] → [[Armbar from Mount]] → [[Cross Collar Choke]].

## What Is the Most Effective BJJ Submission?

The **[[Rear Naked Choke]]** is widely considered the most effective submission in BJJ, with approximately 80% success rate at advanced level. It works in both gi and no-gi, requires minimal strength differential, and is extremely difficult to defend once properly applied from [[Back Control]]. Blood chokes cause unconsciousness in 8–13 seconds, giving the opponent little time to mount a successful escape after the choke is locked.

**Top 5 most effective submissions overall:**
1. [[Rear Naked Choke]] — 80% (back control)
2. [[Triangle Choke]] — 75% (guard/top positions)
3. [[Armbar Finish]] — 73% (available from every position)
4. [[Kimura]] — 70% (side control, half guard, turtle)
5. [[High Elbow Guillotine]] — 68% (guard, scrambles)

## What Are the Main BJJ Submission Categories?

BJJ submissions fall into four primary categories, each attacking different anatomical targets:

### Chokes (Strangulations)

Chokes are the highest-percentage finishing category in BJJ, accounting for approximately 45% of all competition submissions.

**Blood chokes vs. air chokes:** Blood chokes (e.g., [[Rear Naked Choke]], [[Triangle Choke]]) compress the carotid arteries, causing unconsciousness in 8–13 seconds. They are safe when released promptly, carry lower injury risk, and are far more effective in competition. Air chokes restrict the airway — they are slower, more painful, harder to finish, and carry higher injury risk. BJJ training prioritizes blood chokes.

**Key characteristics:**
- Fastest time to unconsciousness (8–13 seconds for blood chokes)
- Work equally well in gi and no-gi
- Less dependent on size and strength differentials
- Difficult to "tough out" compared to joint locks

**Primary choke types:**
- Rear strangulations: [[Rear Naked Choke]], [[Bow and Arrow Choke]]
- Front strangulations: [[Guillotine Choke]], [[Darce Choke]], [[Anaconda Choke]]
- Gi-specific: [[Cross Collar Choke]], [[Loop Choke]], [[Baseball Bat Choke]]
- Hybrid: [[Arm Triangle Progression]], [[Triangle Choke]]

### Arm Locks (Upper Body Joint Attacks)

Arm locks target the elbow joint primarily, comprising roughly 30% of competition submissions. They're available from nearly every position.

**Key characteristics:**
- More time to tap (2–5 seconds) before injury versus chokes
- Require precise angle and hip engagement
- Can be escaped by strong opponents if control is incomplete
- Natural connections to other submissions in chains

**Primary arm lock types:**
- Straight armbars: [[Armbar Finish]], [[Armbar from Guard]], [[Armbar from Mount]]
- Bent arm locks: [[Kimura]], [[Americana]], [[Omoplata]]
- Opportunistic: [[Flying Armbar]]

### Leg Locks (Lower Body Joint Attacks)

Leg locks now account for 15–20% of high-level no-gi submissions, with that percentage increasing annually.

**Key characteristics:**
- Limited pain warning before structural damage — particularly heel hooks
- Require specialized positional systems ([[Ashi Garami]], [[Saddle]])
- IBJJF-restricted at lower belt levels (heel hooks banned below black belt)
- Steep learning curve; high injury risk if practiced incorrectly

**Primary leg lock types:**
- Heel hooks: [[Inside Heel Hook]], [[Outside Heel Hook]]
- Straight ankle locks: [[Straight Footlock]]
- Knee attacks: [[Kneebar]], [[Calf Slicer]]

### Shoulder Locks

Shoulder locks represent 5–10% of finishes but offer unique advantages in specific positions.

**Key characteristics:**
- Often function as control positions as much as pure submissions
- High injury risk if applied explosively
- Natural transitions to other submission categories

**Primary shoulder lock types:**
- [[Kimura]] (also functions as arm lock)
- [[Omoplata]] (hybrid shoulder/arm attack)
- [[Williams Shoulder Lock]]
- [[Twister Finish]] (extreme spinal/shoulder attack — advanced only)

## Which BJJ Submissions Are Available from Each Position?

### What BJJ Submissions Work from Mount Position?

[[Mount]] is considered the premier submission position in traditional BJJ, offering 4 points and dominant control with multiple simultaneous attack vectors.

**Overall submission rate from mount:** 25–30% in competition, increasing significantly at higher belt levels.

| Submission | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced | Key Setup |
|-----------|----------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| [[Armbar from Mount]] | 50% | 65% | 80% | Break posture, isolate arm, swing leg over head |
| [[Cross Collar Choke]] | 45% | 60% | 75% | High mount, deep cross collar grips |
| [[Americana]] | 40% | 55% | 70% | Pin hand to mat, figure-four grip, rotate |
| [[Ezekiel Choke]] | 35% | 50% | 65% | Forearm under neck, opposite hand grips own sleeve |
| [[Triangle Choke]] | 30% | 45% | 65% | High mount, arm across neck, lock triangle |

**Expert insight (John Danaher):** "Mount submissions succeed because they offer multiple attack vectors simultaneously. Focus on establishing high mount and threatening the cross collar choke — this forces arms high, opening the armbar. Use one threat to set up another. This systematic approach is the essence of effective mount attacks."

### What BJJ Submissions Work from Back Control?

[[Back Control]] offers the highest submission success rate of any position (35–40% in competition). The attacking position controls the opponent's back and hips, limiting defensive options while threatening the neck and arms simultaneously.

| Submission | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced | Key Setup |
|-----------|----------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| [[Rear Naked Choke]] | 70% | 80% | 90% | Secure both hooks, slide arm under chin |
| [[Bow and Arrow Choke]] | 55% | 70% | 85% | Secure collar grip, control near leg, extend |
| [[Armbar from Back]] | 45% | 60% | 75% | Isolate defending arm, transition to armbar |
| [[Short Choke]] | 40% | 55% | 70% | Palm-to-palm grip behind head, squeeze elbows |

**Expert insight (Gordon Ryan):** "Back control is the king of positions because the opponent cannot see your attacks. I focus on the body triangle to remove lower body defense, then systematically attack the neck. If they defend high, the armbar opens. If they defend the armbar, the neck reopens. Patience — maintain position and let them exhaust themselves defending."

### What BJJ Submissions Work from Side Control?

[[Side Control]] offers solid control with opportunity to transition to more dominant positions. Submissions generally require more setup than from mount or back.

| Submission | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced | Key Setup |
|-----------|----------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| [[Kimura from Side Control]] | 50% | 65% | 78% | Isolate far arm, figure-four grip, break from mat |
| [[Americana]] | 45% | 60% | 75% | Near arm pinned to mat, figure-four, rotate |
| [[Arm Triangle Progression]] | 40% | 58% | 73% | Trap near arm and head, lock figure-four |
| [[Paper Cutter Choke]] | 35% | 50% | 68% | Deep collar grip, drive forearm across neck |

**Expert insight (Eddie Bravo):** "From side control, the Kimura is king — not just as a submission, but as a system. You can sweep with it, take the back, transition to mount, or finish the shoulder lock. The Kimura trap from side control is one of the highest-percentage control systems in all of grappling."

### What BJJ Submissions Work from Guard?

Guard positions ([[Closed Guard]], [[Open Guard]], [[Half Guard]]) offer the most submission variety — 20+ options — though generally lower success rates (15–25%) due to being the bottom position.

| [[Armbar from Guard]] | 50% | 65% | 80% | Control arm, open guard, pivot for armbar |
| [[High Elbow Guillotine]] | 45% | 60% | 75% | Snap down, wrap neck, close guard |
| [[Kimura from Guard]] | 40% | 55% | 72% | Secure figure-four grip, break posture, extend arm |

**Additional guard submissions:** [[Loop Choke]], [[Cross Collar Choke]], [[De La Riva Guard]] attacks, [[Spider Guard]] attacks

### What BJJ Submissions Work from Leg Entanglements?

Modern leg lock positions ([[Ashi Garami]], [[50-50 Guard]], [[Saddle]]) have revolutionized no-gi grappling. These positions offer high finishing rates but require specialized knowledge and carry significant injury risk.

| Submission | Beginner | Intermediate | Advanced | Key Setup |
|-----------|----------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| [[Inside Heel Hook]] | 30% | 55% | 80% | Secure inside position, control legs, rotate heel |
| [[Outside Heel Hook]] | 25% | 50% | 75% | Outside ashi position, trap leg, rotate heel |

**Safety warning:** Leg locks, particularly heel hooks, have limited pain warning before catastrophic knee damage. Never practice heel hooks without qualified instruction. Always tap early to any leg attack.

**Expert insight (John Danaher):** "Start with straight ankle locks. Progress to kneebars at brown belt minimum. Only explore heel hooks under expert supervision at black belt with trustworthy training partners."

## How Do BJJ Submission Chains Work?

**Submission chains** exploit a fundamental principle: defending one attack creates the perfect position for the next. The most effective submission artists don't rely on single techniques — they create sequences where each defensive movement opens a new attack.

**Why chains succeed:** The opponent cannot simultaneously defend their neck, both arms, and their base. By pressuring one attack, you force a choice, and each choice exposes another target. Chain success rates dramatically exceed single-technique rates:
- Single technique success: 55–70%
- Two-technique chain: 75–85%
- Three-technique chain: 85–95%

### The Classic Guard Triangle: Triangle → Armbar → Omoplata

The foundational submission chain from [[Closed Guard]].

1. Attack [[Triangle Choke]] — opponent defends by posturing up and pulling trapped arm free
2. Transition to [[Armbar Finish]] — opponent defends by turning toward you and stacking
3. Transition to [[Omoplata]] — opponent must now defend shoulder lock or be swept

**Training priority:** White belts should drill this chain every session until transitions become automatic.

### The Kimura System: Kimura → Guillotine → Armbar

Works from [[Side Control]], [[Half Guard]], and [[Turtle]].

1. Secure [[Kimura]] grip — opponent defends by keeping elbow tight to body
2. Switch to [[Guillotine Choke]] — opponent lifts head to escape
3. Transition to [[Armbar Finish]] — opponent extends arm trying to defend

**Expert insight (Gordon Ryan):** "The Kimura grip is the skeleton key of BJJ. I can sweep, pass, take the back, or submit directly from this single grip. When I grab the Kimura, my opponent knows they're in danger, but defending it opens up five other attacks."

### The Gi Choke Series: Cross Collar → Loop Choke → Ezekiel

Dominates from [[Mount]] using collar grips.

1. Threaten [[Cross Collar Choke]] — opponent blocks collar hand
2. Switch to [[Loop Choke]] — opponent turns to escape
3. Transition to [[Ezekiel Choke]] — opponent lifts head to breathe

### The Leg Lock System: Inside Heel Hook → Outside Heel Hook → Kneebar

The modern leg lock meta — brown/black belt only with experienced partners.

1. Attack [[Inside Heel Hook]] — opponent defends by rotating knee inward
2. Transition to [[Outside Heel Hook]] — opponent pulls leg back
3. Finish with [[Kneebar]] — opponent straightens leg to escape heel hooks

### The Back Attack Sequence: Rear Naked Choke → Armbar → Bow and Arrow

From [[Back Control]], three high-percentage finishes from one position.

1. Attack [[Rear Naked Choke]] — opponent defends neck with hands
2. Isolate defending arm for [[Armbar from Back]] — opponent clasps hands
3. Transition to [[Bow and Arrow Choke]] — opponent turns to defend armbar

IBJJF rules restrict certain submissions at lower belt levels due to injury risk.

| Straight armbar | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

| Toe hold | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Kneebar | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |

**Key rule notes:**
- Spinal locks and neck cranks are banned at all belt levels in IBJJF
- Knee reaping (placing the knee across the opponent's body in leg entanglements) is banned at all levels
- No-gi and submission-only formats often use different rule sets — verify with each event
- ADCC and EBI competitions generally allow heel hooks at all levels

## Which BJJ Submissions Should Each Belt Level Learn?

### What BJJ Submissions Should White Belts Learn First?

**Focus:** Fundamental submissions from dominant positions with simple mechanics.

**Priority order for first 2 years:**
1. [[Rear Naked Choke]] — Highest percentage, simplest mechanics, immediate utility
2. [[Americana]] — Teaches bent-arm control principles from mount and side control
3. [[Triangle Choke]] — Classic guard submission, foundation for chains
4. [[Armbar from Mount]] — Versatile arm attack, teaches hip engagement
5. [[Cross Collar Choke]] — Fundamental gi technique from mount
6. [[Guillotine Choke]] — Defensive counter to takedown attempts
7. [[Straight Footlock]] — Entry into leg attack systems, legal at all levels

**Learning approach:** Static drilling → positional sparring → live application. Focus on control before submission. Minimum 100 reps per technique before attempting in live rolling.

**Common mistake:** Attempting low-percentage submissions (flying triangles, spinning armbars) before foundational techniques are reliable at 50%+ success rate in drilling.

### Blue Belt Priority (Years 2–4)

**Focus:** Submission chains, increased versatility, developing personal game.

**Add to arsenal:**
1. [[Kimura]] system — Grip-based control and multi-option attacking
2. [[Omoplata from Guard]] — Guard retention and sweeping option
3. [[Darce Choke]] — Front headlock position attacks
4. [[Arm Triangle Progression]] — No-gi high percentage
5. [[Loop Choke]] — Gi-specific opportunistic attack
6. [[Armbar from Guard]] — Guard game development

**Development goals:** 2–3 reliable submissions from each major position; begin recognizing submission windows 2–3 moves earlier.

### Purple Belt Priority (Years 4–7)

**Focus:** Systematic approach, teaching capacity, refining personal game.

**Add to arsenal:**
1. [[Anaconda Choke]] — Front headlock system completion
2. [[Baseball Bat Choke]] — Gi-specific control positions
3. [[Kneebar]] — Expanded leg attack system
4. [[Bow and Arrow Choke]] — Back attack variety
5. Advanced guard submissions from [[De La Riva Guard]], [[Spider Guard]]

**Development goals:** 70%+ success rate on fundamental submissions in drilling; proven competition submissions; ability to chain 3+ techniques reliably.

### Brown/Black Belt Priority (Years 7+)

**Focus:** Complete system integration, innovation, situational mastery.

**Add to arsenal:**
1. [[Inside Heel Hook]] — Modern leg lock game (safety critical — supervised only)
2. [[Outside Heel Hook]] — Leg lock system completion
3. [[Twister Finish]] — Advanced control positions
4. Situational: [[Flying Armbar]], [[Gogoplata]]
5. Competition-specific traps and setups

**Development goals:** 80%+ success rate on primary submissions; recognized personal system; ability to teach complete submission framework.

## How Do You Practice BJJ Submissions Safely?

Submissions involve deliberate attacks on joints and blood flow — safety must be paramount at all stages.

### Universal Safety Principles

**Tap early and tap often:**
- Tap immediately when caught — never "tough out" joint locks
- Verbal taps ("tap") are equally valid as physical taps
- Ego injuries heal slower than joints

**Progressive application:**
- Apply submissions slowly over 3–5 seconds of progressive pressure
- Never "spike" submissions with explosive force
- Fully control the position before applying submission pressure

**Partner trust:**
- Only practice dangerous techniques (heel hooks, neck cranks, spinal attacks) with experienced, trustworthy partners
- Release immediately upon any tap
- Communicate your experience level before rolling

### Are Heel Hooks Dangerous in BJJ?

Yes — heel hooks are among the most dangerous submissions in grappling. Reasons:

- **Limited pain warning:** The knee ligaments can rupture before significant pain is felt
- **Rapid damage:** A full heel hook can cause complete ACL/MCL destruction in under one second
- **Escape risk:** Attempting to "roll out" of a heel hook often causes the injury
- **IBJJF banned below black belt** for these exact reasons

**Safe heel hook practice:**
- Only with brown/black belt partners who understand the danger
- Tap at the first sensation of pressure, not when it hurts
- Never try to escape by rotation once caught — tap immediately
- Start from controlled static positions before adding movement

### Position-Specific Safety

**Arm locks:** Hyperextension injuries occur rapidly. Fully control the arm before applying pressure. Never "jump" to armbars without hip control in place.

**Shoulder locks ([[Kimura]], [[Americana]], [[Omoplata]]):** Apply progressive pressure over 3–5 seconds. Watch for pain signals. Shoulder surgery requires 6–9 months recovery.

**Neck cranks:** Distinguish blood chokes (safe) from neck cranks (dangerous). If you feel spinal pressure rather than carotid pressure, adjust or release immediately.

## Expert Strategic Insights

### John Danaher: Position Before Submission

"Submissions are not isolated techniques — they are systematic outcomes of positional dominance. Study positions before submissions. A perfectly executed submission attempt from poor position will fail. A mediocre submission attempt from dominant position will often succeed.

From any given position, there exists a hierarchy of threats. Learn to attack the highest-percentage threat first, using its defense to set up the second threat. This is 'systematic progression' — each defensive action by your opponent creates the setup for your next attack."

### Gordon Ryan: Depth Over Breadth

"Pick three submissions and make them world-class. I'd rather have three submissions at 90% effectiveness than thirty at 50%. My primary attacks are the rear naked choke, inside heel hook, and armbar. Everything I do creates one of those three scenarios.

Competition data shows athletes who finish matches have drilled their primary submissions at least 5,000 times. Not practiced — drilled with perfect technique. That's the difference between hoping for a submission and knowing you'll get it."

### Eddie Bravo: Attack From Everywhere

"The traditional approach is: get position, get submission. My approach: attack from anywhere, always. Some of the highest-percentage submissions in my system come from positions traditional BJJ considers neutral or disadvantageous. The rubber guard generates omoplatas and triangles from positions where opponents think they're safe.

Don't let belt level tell you what submissions you can or can't learn. If you're going to spend ten years training a technique, start now. But drilling without innovation is stagnation. Find your balance."

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best BJJ submission for beginners?
The [[Rear Naked Choke]] is the best submission for beginners. It has the highest success rate (70%+ even at beginner level), works in gi and no-gi, requires minimal setup, and teaches back control maintenance. Second recommendation: [[Americana]] from mount, which teaches fundamental bent-arm control principles applicable to many other submissions.

### How long does it take to learn BJJ submissions?
Basic mechanics of a submission can be learned in one class. Reliable application in live rolling typically requires 3–6 months of consistent drilling (100+ quality reps plus positional sparring). A submission becoming part of your competitive game generally takes 1–2 years of focused development. True mastery — hitting it against high-level resistance on demand — is a black belt skill.

### What is the fastest BJJ submission?
The [[Rear Naked Choke]] (blood choke) causes unconsciousness in 8–13 seconds once fully locked. Among joint locks, the [[Inside Heel Hook]] can cause serious knee damage almost instantly. This is precisely why heel hooks require tapping at first pressure rather than waiting for pain.

### What is a submission rate in BJJ competition?
Submission rate is the percentage of matches finished by submission rather than points or decision. From [[Back Control]], the submission rate is 35–40% in high-level competition. From [[Mount]], approximately 25–30%. Elite submission specialists like Gordon Ryan achieve 50–70%+ career submission rates through systematic technique and chain attacks.

### What is the difference between a blood choke and an air choke?
**Blood chokes** (e.g., [[Rear Naked Choke]], [[Triangle Choke]]) compress the carotid arteries, cutting blood flow to the brain. They cause unconsciousness in 8–13 seconds, carry low injury risk when released promptly, and are preferred in competition. **Air chokes** restrict the airway — they are slower to take effect, more painful, harder to finish against a resisting opponent, and riskier. BJJ training focuses on blood chokes.

### What BJJ submissions are banned in IBJJF competition?
IBJJF bans: heel hooks (below black belt), knee reaping at all levels, toe holds (below purple belt), kneebars (below brown belt), and spinal locks/neck cranks at all levels. Full details in the IBJJF legal submissions table above.

### How do I escape a rear naked choke in BJJ?
Primary defenses: (1) **Protect before it's locked** — tuck your chin the moment you feel an arm approaching your neck, (2) **Two-on-one hand fighting** — grip the choking arm with both hands and pull down while turning into the opponent, (3) **Disrupt the body lock** — turn into the opponent and face them to break the hooks. Prevention is most effective — don't allow back control in the first place by defending aggressively before the back is taken.

## Conclusion: Building Your Submission Game

A systematic submission framework accelerates development more than accumulating isolated techniques. The practitioners who progress fastest treat submission learning as a strategic exercise: prioritize high-percentage techniques from their most common positions, drill chains rather than isolated attacks, and progressively expand their arsenal as positional game matures.

**Key takeaways:**

1. **Position determines options** — Learn which submissions are available from each position; prioritize highest-percentage options first.
2. **Chains beat single techniques** — The triangle–armbar–omoplata chain succeeds at 85–95% because defending one opens the next.
3. **Success rates progress with skill** — White belt rates (40–50%) progress to black belt rates (75–85%) through drilling and systematic study.
4. **Safety enables longevity** — Aggressive training without safety protocols creates injuries that halt progress for months.
5. **Specialization beats generalization** — Three world-class submissions beat thirty mediocre ones.

**Your next steps:**
- Identify your three most common positions and learn the top two submissions from each
- Drill the triangle–armbar–omoplata chain 100 times this month
- Track submission attempts and success rates in training
- Study video of high-level competitors executing your chosen submissions
