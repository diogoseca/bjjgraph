# BJJ Escapes: Your Survival Toolkit

Here's the truth about Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu: you'll spend a lot of time in bad positions, especially early on. The difference between someone who quits after three months and someone who earns their blue belt? Escapes.

**You cannot attack if you cannot survive.** Escapes are the foundation of everything else. Master them first, attack later.

---

## The Defensive Hierarchy

When you're stuck in a bad position, your priorities are clear:

1. **Survive** - Defend submissions first (you can't escape if you're tapping)
2. **Create space** - Use frames and movement to prevent them consolidating
3. **Escape** - Get out of the bad position
4. **Recover** - Return to guard or neutral
5. **Counter** - Turn defense into offense (advanced)

The biggest mistake beginners make? Trying to skip straight to step 5. Stay alive first, then worry about being fancy.

## Mount Escapes

Mount is the 4-point position where someone's sitting on your chest. It sucks, but it's escapable.

### Essential Mount Escapes

**[[Bridge and Roll]]** (Upa Escape)
The first mount escape everyone learns, and it actually works. Trap their arm and foot on the same side, bridge explosively, roll them over. The timing window is brief—catch them when they're posting or reaching. Success rates improve dramatically with practice: beginners land it about 45% of the time, advanced players hit 75%.

**[[Hip Escape]]** (Elbow-Knee Escape)
The most reliable mount escape, period. Frame on their hips, shrimp to create space, insert your knee. Repeat until you recover half guard or closed guard. It's not flashy, but it works even when they see it coming. Most mount escapes eventually involve some version of this.

**Technical Stand-up**
Sometimes the best escape is getting back to your feet. Create space, turn to your side, and work your way up. Critical for MMA and self-defense situations where staying on the ground is dangerous.

## Side Control Escapes

Side control (3 points) is where you'll spend the most time as a white belt. Good news: it's easier to escape than mount if you know what you're doing.

### Essential Side Control Escapes

**Frame and Shrimp**
The fundamental escape. Inside arm frames their neck, outside arm frames their hip. Shrimp your hips away, create space, insert a knee. Recover to half guard first (easier), then work to closed guard. You'll use this escape in almost every roll for the rest of your BJJ career.

**Bridge and Roll**
Less reliable from side control than from mount, but it works when they're off-balance. Requires an underhook and perfect timing. When it lands, it's spectacular. When it doesn't, you're still in side control—so use sparingly.

**Turtle Transition**
Not technically an escape, but sometimes your only option. Turn toward them and establish turtle position. It's a defensive shell that buys you time. From there, you can stand up or work back to guard. Definitely better than staying flat on your back.

**→ Key concept:** Multiple shrimps are usually necessary. One shrimp rarely creates enough space.

##Back Control Escapes

Back control (4 points) is the most dominant position in BJJ. Escaping requires technique, composure, and systematic defense.

### Surviving Back Control

**Step 1: Defend the Choke**
If they sink in a rear naked choke, nothing else matters. Two-on-one grip fighting. Chin down. Fight the hands before you worry about the position. Once the choke is locked, it's over—so defend early.

**Step 2: Escape the Position**
Hip escape toward the bottom hook (the one lower on your body). Strip the hooks as you move. Get your shoulders to the mat. This breaks their control and lets you recover guard or create a scramble.

**Advanced: Granby Roll**
The inverted rolling escape from back control. Looks impressive, works consistently once you learn it, requires flexibility and lots of practice. Purple belt and up territory.

**→ Reality check:** Back escapes have lower success rates than mount or side control escapes. The position is that dominant. Focus on preventing back takes in the first place.

## Guard Pass Defense

The best escape is not getting passed in the first place.

### Guard Retention Essentials

**Distance Management**
Stay at the right range—too close and they'll pressure pass you, too far and they'll speed pass you. Feet on hips is your friend. Frames are your friend. Letting them settle into passing grips is not your friend.

**The Three Stages of Defense**
1. Early - Prevent them establishing passing grips (highest success)
2. Middle - Recover to half guard when partially passed (medium success)
3. Late - Accept side control and immediately work escapes (lowest success)

Moral of the story: defend early. Recovery gets exponentially harder with each stage.

**Key Guards for Retention**
[[Half Guard]] is your checkpoint position—if you can't save closed guard, save half guard. [[Knee Shield]] creates a strong defensive frame. [[Butterfly Guard]] keeps you mobile and offensive.

## Submission Escapes

Escaping submissions requires early recognition and immediate action.

### The Critical Ones

**Armbar Defense**
Prevent full extension at all costs. Keep your elbow bent. Rotate your thumb up (hitchhiker). Stack them if possible. Once your arm is fully extended, tapping is the only safe option.

**Triangle Defense**
Posture is everything. Stand up, stack them, create space. If they've already locked the triangle and have your posture broken, you're in serious trouble. Defend the setup, not the finish.

**Rear Naked Choke Defense**
Fight the hands before they lock it. Two hands beat one hand. Create space by turning your head away. Once the choke is locked with proper technique, you have about 3-5 seconds before sleep.

**→ Philosophy:** Submissions are defended in their early stages. If someone has you caught in a fully locked submission, don't be a hero—tap and learn.

## Essential Movements

Every escape uses these fundamental movements. Drill them until they're automatic.

**The Shrimp (Hip Escape)**
The most important movement in BJJ. Turn on your side, push off your bottom foot, drive your hips back. Creates space and angles for every escape. White belts should drill this 50 times per day minimum.

**The Bridge (Upa)**
Explosive hip lift that breaks opponent's base. Power comes from your legs driving through your heels, not your back. Used in mount escapes and certain sweeps.

**Technical Stand-up**
Getting to your feet safely while facing your opponent. One hand posts behind you, opposite leg extended as a barrier. Essential for self-defense and MMA applications.

## What You Need to Learn (By Belt Level)

### White Belt: Survive

Focus on these five escapes. Everything else is noise.

1. **Hip escape (the shrimp)** - Drill it daily
2. **Bridge and roll** from mount
3. **Frame and shrimp** from side control
4. **Rear naked choke defense** (hand fighting)
5. **Technical stand-up**

Master these before worrying about anything fancy. Survival first.

### Blue Belt: Build Systems

Now you're ready for complete escape systems:

- Full mount escape toolkit (multiple options based on their reactions)
- Side control ghost escapes and advanced shrimping
- Back escape with Granby rolls
- Guard retention concepts (prevent passes before they happen)
- Submission defenses for the major submissions

### Purple Belt and Up: Prevention

At this level, you should rarely stay in bad positions. Your escapes are automatic, invisible, and often prevent the bad position from being established in the first place. You're thinking three moves ahead.

## The Reality of Escapes

Here's what experienced grapplers know: escapes are hard. They require more technique than attacks, more energy than control positions, and more mental composure than almost anything else in BJJ.

A high-level BJJ player isn't someone who never ends up in bad positions. It's someone who escapes them so reliably that opponents get frustrated and make mistakes.

**Your escapes should make your opponents work harder than you do.**

That's the goal. Master these fundamentals, drill them until they're reflex, and you'll be the training partner everyone struggles to control.
