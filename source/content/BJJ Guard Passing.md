# BJJ Guard Passing: The Essential Skill

Guard passing is where matches are won. You can't get to mount, side control, or back control without passing the guard first. In competition, passing scores 3 points—but more importantly, it establishes the positional dominance that leads to submissions. The truth? Most BJJ matches are decided here, in the passing game, not in the submissions that come after.

## Pressure vs Speed: The Two Schools

Every guard pass falls into one of two categories, though elite passers blur the lines constantly.

### Pressure Passing

Watch Bernardo Faria against anyone. He makes them carry his weight for five minutes straight. [[Crossface Control|Crossface]], shoulder pressure, grinding control—he pins them down, exhausts their frames, makes breathing feel like a chore, and passes when their defensive structure collapses from fatigue.

Pressure passing works best against aggressive guard players who want to move and attack. Your weight becomes their problem. You're working, but they're working harder. The energy cost is medium for you, catastrophic for them.

The core passes: [[Smash Pass]], [[Stack Pass]], [[Knee Cut Pass]] with heavy pressure, [[Body Lock Pass]]. Often starts from [[Combat Base]] or [[Headquarters Position]].

### Speed Passing

The Mendes brothers move like they're on fast-forward. [[Toreando Pass|Toreando]], [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drag]], cartwheel passes—they're past your guard before your brain registers what happened. Speed passing is the roadrunner approach: move faster than they can react, pass before they can establish defensive frames.

Works best against slow or heavy opponents, people who rely on strength over timing. The energy cost is high—explosive bursts required—but when it works, it's devastatingly effective.

The core passes: [[Toreando Pass]], [[Leg Drag Pass]], [[Leg Weave Pass]]. Often used from [[Standing Guard|standing]] or [[Open Guard Top|open guard top]].

### The Hybrid Reality

Gordon Ryan and Roger Gracie don't pick one style and stick with it. They read the situation. Pressure when the opponent is aggressive and trying to attack. Speed when the opponent is slow and reactive. The key isn't knowing more passes—it's reading which pass the situation demands.

## The Passes That Actually Matter

### Knee Cut Pass

The most fundamental pass in modern BJJ. Works gi and no-gi, works against most guards, chains into everything else. If you're learning one pressure pass first, make it this one.

You're cutting your knee across their thigh like a blade while using shoulder pressure on their upper body. Control the far leg, drive your weight forward, slide through to [[Side Control Top|side control]]. The [[Knee Cut Position]] is your checkpoint—if you can hold it even for a second, the pass is nearly guaranteed.

Success comes from understanding it's not one explosive movement—it's controlled pressure with your weight distributed correctly. Most failed knee cuts happen because people try to speed through instead of grinding through. When it fails, it's usually because you didn't control the far leg or you gave up the [[Underhook Control|underhook]].

### Smash Pass

The bread and butter of [[Half Guard Top|half guard]] passing. Bernardo Faria built a world championship game around this pass and its variations.

Crossface across their face, underhook on the far side, drive your shoulder pressure into their jaw. Flatten them completely, make them deeply uncomfortable, then clear the trapped leg when their defensive frames weaken. Finish in [[Side Control Top|side control]].

The psychological component matters here—people hate being smashed. They make mistakes trying to escape the discomfort, and that's exactly when you complete the pass. Also called "headquarters pass" or "crossface pass" depending on who's teaching. See [[Headquarters Position]] for the control position this often starts from.

### Stack Pass

Fold them in half and walk around their legs. Works from [[Closed Guard Top|closed guard]], [[Open Guard Top|open guard]], [[Half Guard Top|half guard]].

The key is stacking them HIGH—legs near their face, weight driving down through their hips into the mat. Most people don't stack high enough, and that's why it fails. Once properly stacked, their legs lose leverage and you can walk to either side to [[Side Control Top|side control]]. But this is a committed pass. You can't half-ass a stack—either stack them completely or choose a different pass. See [[Stack Defense]] for what your opponent is trying to stop.

### Toreando Pass

Control their pants or ankles, push their legs to one side, quickly circle to the opposite side. The name means "bullfighter pass" and the mechanic is literally the same—move the cape one way, step the other.

Timing matters more than strength here. Fast, clean toreandos are nearly unstoppable. Slow, muscly toreandos get swept. You're using misdirection and speed, not force.

### Leg Drag Pass

Grab one leg, drag it across their body, backstep and circle to their back-exposed side. Often leads directly to [[Back Control Top|back takes]] if executed correctly.

This pass is everywhere in modern no-gi competition. Gordon Ryan has built much of his dominance around leg drag systems. The [[Leg Drag Position]] creates back exposure and makes defending multiple attacks nearly impossible. The success rate is high because even when the pass "fails," you often get [[Back Control Top|the back]] anyway. Works great from [[Open Guard Top|open guard]] or [[Butterfly Guard Top|butterfly guard]].

### Body Lock Pass

Lock your hands around their torso, eliminate their leg attacks entirely, drive forward. Simple, controlling, highly effective once you understand the grip mechanics. See [[Body Lock]] for the control position.

Best against aggressive guard players trying to attack your legs with [[De La Riva Guard]], [[Spider Guard]], or [[Lasso Guard]]. Once you have the body lock secured, they've lost most of their offensive options. You're just controlling their entire torso.

## Guard-Specific Strategies

[[Closed Guard Top|Closed guard]] must be opened first, then passed immediately. The mistake beginners make is opening it, pausing to think, and watching it close again. Choose your pass before you open the guard. Most modern competitors stand to pass closed guard—[[Toreando Pass|toreando]] or [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drag]] from standing. [[Combat Base]] is slower but safer.

[[Half Guard Top|Half guard]] is where you'll spend half your passing time (pun intended). Control the [[Underhook Battle|underhook battle]]. Whoever gets the [[Underhook Control|underhook]] controls the position. If they have it, your pass gets significantly harder. If you have it, the [[Smash Pass|smash pass]] becomes available. [[Headquarters Position]] is your control checkpoint—knee on belly of the trapped leg, excellent base, multiple options from there.

[[Open Guard Top|Open guard]] is the widest category. Dozens of variations exist—[[Butterfly Guard]], [[Spider Guard]], [[De La Riva Guard]], [[Lasso Guard]], [[Collar Sleeve Guard]]. Universal principles: control distance (don't let them extend AND retract freely), win the grip fight (their grips enable their guard), and stay mobile or stay heavy (pick one, commit to it). The truth is [[Knee Cut Pass|knee cut]], [[Toreando Pass|toreando]], and [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drag]] work against most open guards. The best pass is often just the one you're most comfortable with.

## How Experts Actually Pass

Individual passes are useful. Passing systems are unbeatable.

Watch high-level matches and you'll see this: the passer starts with one approach, the guard player defends, the passer immediately switches to a different pass without resetting. That's the system—not isolated techniques, but a framework of connected options.

**Pressure system**: Establish [[Body Lock|body lock]] or [[Headquarters Position|headquarters]]. Apply [[Crossface Control|crossface]] pressure. Read their reaction. If they turn away, take [[Back Control Top|the back]]. If they frame and attempt reguard, [[Knee Cut Pass|knee cut]] through. If they try to sit up, [[Stack Pass|stack]] them. Never stop pressuring. The system works because every defensive response leads to a different pass, all from the same pressure foundation.

**Speed system**: Control the legs with passing grips. Move constantly. [[Toreando Pass|Toreando]] blocked? Switch to [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drag]]. Leg drag blocked? Switch to [[Leg Weave Pass|leg weave]]. Keep them defending, never let them settle. The system works because they're reacting instead of attacking. See [[Guard Retention]] for what they're trying to do.

**Headquarters system**: Establish [[Headquarters Position|headquarters position]] from [[Half Guard Top|half guard]]. From there: [[Smash Pass|smash pass]], [[Knee Cut Pass|knee cut]], [[Back Take Generic|back take]], various leg attacks like [[Straight Ankle Lock]] or [[Kneebar]]. Headquarters is the control position. Once there, passing is just picking the right finish based on their reaction. Gordon Ryan's primary half guard approach.

## What to Learn First

White belts should learn one pressure pass and one speed pass. That's it. Recommended: [[Knee Cut Pass]] for pressure, [[Toreando Pass]] for speed. Master these two and you can pass most white and blue belt guards from [[Open Guard Top|open guard]] or after opening [[Closed Guard Top|closed guard]]. Add more passes later—foundation first, variety later.

Blue belts should develop both styles completely. Pressure system ([[Knee Cut Pass|knee cut]], [[Smash Pass|smash]], [[Stack Pass|stack]], [[Body Lock Pass|body lock]]) and speed system ([[Toreando Pass|toreando]], [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drag]], [[Leg Weave Pass|leg weave]]). Understand when to use each. Drill transitions between passes. Learn to read which pass the situation demands. Study [[Headquarters Position]] and [[Combat Base]] as control positions.

Purple belts and up should specialize based on body type and personality. Tall and lanky? Probably speed passing with [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drags]] and [[Toreando Pass|toreandos]]. Stocky and strong? Probably pressure passing with [[Smash Pass|smashes]] and [[Stack Pass|stacks]]. Medium build? Probably hybrid. But here's the thing: elite passers make their style work against everyone. Faria pressure-passes people who should be pressure-proof. The Mendes brothers speed-pass people who should be too fast to speed-pass.

## Common Mistakes

Weak consolidation is the most common failure. You passed, but didn't secure [[Side Control Top|side control]] completely, so they [[Guard Recovery|reguard]]. The pass isn't done until you've established stable control. Don't celebrate until you're actually safe.

Fighting the strength means attacking where they're strongest instead of passing to their weak side. If their right side frames are solid, pass left. Seems obvious, but in the moment people forget. Read their [[Guard Retention]] patterns and exploit the weak side.

Predictable patterns kill your passing. Using the same pass every time trains your opponents to defend it specifically. Mix [[Smash Pass|pressure]] and [[Toreando Pass|speed]]. Keep them guessing what's coming.

Poor posture in [[Closed Guard Top|closed guard]] gets you swept or submitted before you even attempt the pass. Back straight, hips back, structural posture maintained. This is fundamental. See [[Base Maintenance]] and [[Posture Control]] for the principles.

Rushing is trying to pass faster than your control allows. Establish control at each stage, then advance. Slow is smooth, smooth is fast. This cliché exists because it's true.

## Training Methods

Positional sparring is the fastest way to improve your passing. Start in the guard you want to practice passing, set a 5-minute timer, and the only outcomes are: you pass or you get swept. Do this for 3-4 rounds per session and your passing will improve faster than any drilling could achieve.

Progressive resistance builds the movement pattern: partner lets you pass (learn the mechanics) → light resistance (practice under pressure) → moderate resistance (realistic training) → full competition resistance (test yourself).

Flow passing at lower intensity builds muscle memory and teaches you to read reactions without the chaos of full sparring. Useful, but can't replace real resistance training.

The best submission artists in the world are often the best passers. Why? Because submissions come from passed guards. You can't finish people from their guard (with rare exceptions like [[Triangle Choke|triangles from guard]]). You have to pass to [[Side Control Top|side control]], [[Mount Top|mount]], or [[Back Control Top|back control]] first.

Elite passers create no-win scenarios for their opponents. Block the [[Knee Cut Pass|knee cut]]? They switch to [[Toreando Pass|toreando]]. Block the toreando? They switch to [[Leg Drag Pass|leg drag]]. Block the leg drag? They're already past your guard. Make opponents exhausted and frustrated just trying to keep you in their guard. That's when passing becomes easy—when they're tired of defending and you're just getting started.
