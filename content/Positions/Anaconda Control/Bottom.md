<section id="overview" class="content-section">

**Anaconda Control Bottom** is a highly dangerous defensive position where the opponent has locked a gable or figure-four grip around your neck and near-side arm from a front headlock. **Average time to escape or submission: 10–30 seconds. Act immediately.** This is primarily a blood choke — unconsciousness can occur in 8–12 seconds once fully locked, often before significant pain is felt.

The top player's chest drives downward pressure into your trapped shoulder while their body is positioned perpendicular to yours, restricting breathing and mobility. The bottom player's survival hierarchy has three priorities: (1) prevent the choke from tightening, (2) relieve pressure on the neck, (3) create space to escape to [[Positions/Turtle]], [[Positions/Open Guard]], or [[Positions/Closed Guard]].

This position most commonly arises from scrambles out of [[Positions/Turtle]], failed takedown attempts, or transitions from other [[Positions/Front Headlock]] positions. Understanding the mechanics of how the [[Transitions/Anaconda Choke]] works is essential for defending it — the choke tightens as the top player pulls their choking arm's elbow toward their own hip while driving chest pressure into your trapped shoulder. Time is critical: once the position is fully locked, escape becomes exponentially harder.

</section>

<section id="safety" class="content-section">

## Safety and Tap Protocol: Anaconda Choke Is a Fast Blood Choke

**The anaconda choke is one of the fastest submissions in BJJ.** As a blood choke targeting the carotid arteries, it can cause unconsciousness in 8–12 seconds once fully locked — faster than most practitioners expect, and often without strong pain signals preceding loss of consciousness.

**Mandatory tap protocol:**
- **Tap immediately** when you feel the choke tightening and cannot create neck space with your free hand
- **Do not wait** for pain confirmation — the anaconda frequently causes unconsciousness before pain becomes acute
- Tap verbally ("tap, tap") or physically (two hand slaps on the mat/opponent) — any clear signal
- If your hand is trapped and you cannot tap, say "tap" loudly
- **In training: err on the side of tapping early** — you gain nothing from going unconscious and lose the opportunity to drill the defense from earlier stages

**For training partners applying the anaconda:** Release immediately on any tap signal. Never slow-roll the finish to "let them escape" — this creates false confidence about escape windows.

</section>

<section id="state-invariants" class="content-section">

## What Is Anaconda Control in BJJ?

**Anaconda Control Bottom** describes the position where an opponent has established the [[Transitions/Anaconda Choke]] setup from a front headlock or [[Positions/Turtle]] position.

**Position invariants:**
- Opponent has secured gable grip or figure-four grip around your neck and near-side arm, with their forearm creating a constricting loop threatening the [[Transitions/Anaconda Choke]]
- Top player's chest drives downward pressure into your trapped shoulder, body positioned perpendicular to yours, using body weight to compress the choke
- Your trapped arm is pinned between your own neck and the opponent's encircling arm, severely limiting your ability to create frames or defensive space
- Your free arm and lower body remain mobile but work against significant mechanical disadvantage — any escape movement must first address the choking pressure


</section>

<section id="anaconda-vs-darce" class="content-section">

## Anaconda vs D'arce vs Guillotine: What's the Difference?

All three are front headlock submissions — understanding each helps you recognize them earlier and apply the correct defense.

| Submission | Arm Threading | Typical Finish | Primary Target |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|----------------|
| **[[Transitions/Anaconda Choke]]** | Under near arm, around neck | Roll-through over shoulder | Carotid arteries (blood choke) |
| **[[Positions/Darce Control]]** | Over near arm, under neck from far side | Static pressure or roll | Carotid arteries (blood choke) |
| **[[Positions/Guillotine Control]]** | Around neck from front (arm-in or arm-out) | Arch back or pull guard | Carotid or airway (depends on variation) |

**Key diagnostic**: If the attacker's arm goes *under* your near-side arm and *around* your neck, it's an anaconda. If their arm goes *over* your near arm and *under* your neck from the other side, it's a D'arce. Both require immediate grip-breaking priority as the first defensive action.

<section id="key-principles" class="content-section">

## How to Survive the Anaconda Choke: Defensive Principles

- **Immediate priority is preventing the choke from tightening** by addressing neck pressure and grip before positional escape
- **Create space** between your neck and opponent's choking arm using your free hand to pull on their wrist or elbow
- **Keep your chin tucked** toward your free-side shoulder to minimize choking surface and protect the carotid arteries
- **Use your free arm to post and create frames** that prevent opponent from fully locking the position
- **Move your hips away** from opponent's control to reduce their ability to drive chest pressure into your shoulder
- **Prevent opponent from stepping over your head** — once complete, the choke becomes extremely difficult to escape
- **Survival hierarchy**: prevent the choke from tightening → create breathing space → escape to better position
- **Do not fight with upper body only** — coordinate hip movement with hand fighting to create escape angles


</section>

<section id="common-mistakes" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Why the Anaconda Choke Is Hard to Escape: Common Mistakes

### 1. Failing to immediately address the choking arm with free hand

- ❌ **Consequence**: Choke tightens rapidly and escape becomes nearly impossible once fully locked
- ✅ **Correction**: Immediately use free hand to pull on opponent's choking wrist or elbow to create space at neck before attempting positional escape

### 2. Turning away from opponent or exposing the back of the neck

- ❌ **Consequence**: Creates additional leverage for opponent to finish choke and eliminates defensive framing options
- ✅ **Correction**: Turn into opponent toward your trapped arm side, keeping chin tucked to free shoulder to minimize choking surface

### 3. Allowing opponent to step over head without resistance or counter-movement

- ❌ **Consequence**: Opponent secures dominant angle and can finish choke with body weight alone
- ✅ **Correction**: Use free hand to post on opponent's hip or leg, preventing the step-over while creating defensive frames

### 4. Fighting with upper body only while keeping lower body stationary

- ❌ **Consequence**: Waste energy without creating meaningful escape opportunities or space
- ✅ **Correction**: Coordinate hip movement away from opponent with upper body hand fighting to systematically create escape angles

### 5. Attempting to pull head straight out of the lock through brute force

- ❌ **Consequence**: Tightens the choke and exhausts energy without addressing the fundamental control problem
- ✅ **Correction**: Focus on creating space at the choking arm's elbow joint while moving hips to change angles rather than pulling directly backward

### 6. Keeping trapped arm rigid and extended

- ❌ **Consequence**: Provides opponent with larger loop to constrict and more leverage to finish submission
- ✅ **Correction**: Keep trapped arm bent and pulled close to body to minimize the space opponent can control with their lock

<section id="training-drills" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Training Drills for Defense

### Anaconda Defense Hand Fighting

Partner establishes anaconda grip from [[Positions/Turtle]] but doesn't finish. Bottom player practices hand fighting to prevent lock from tightening, focusing on pulling opponent's wrist away from neck and creating frames with free arm. Start at 50% resistance and gradually increase. 2-minute rounds.

**Duration**: 2 minutes per round, 5 rounds


### Step-Over Defense Reaction

Partner has anaconda grip and attempts to step over head. Bottom player practices timing defensive posts on opponent's hip, creating frames, and either preventing the step or rolling through with opponent's momentum. Emphasize explosive reaction timing.

**Duration**: 90 seconds per round, 6 rounds


### Progressive Anaconda Escape

Start from various stages of anaconda control: (1) grip established but loose, (2) grip tight but no step-over, (3) step-over initiated, (4) opponent beginning roll. Practice appropriate escape for each stage. Partner provides realistic resistance matched to defender's skill level.

**Duration**: 3 minutes per position stage


### Neck Space Maintenance

Partner has anaconda grip with moderate tightness. Bottom player focuses solely on maintaining space between neck and choking arm using free hand manipulation of opponent's wrist/elbow. Hold space for 30 seconds, rest, repeat. Builds specific endurance for this critical defensive skill.

**Duration**: 30 seconds on, 30 seconds rest, 10 rounds

## Anaconda Defense Quiz: Test Your Knowledge

**Q1**: What is the first priority when caught in anaconda control from bottom?
**A**: The first priority is preventing the choke from tightening — not escaping the position. Use your free hand immediately to grip the opponent's choking wrist or forearm and pull it away from your neck to create space. Only after establishing this defensive hand position should you work on positional escape. Trying to escape first while the choke is tightening is the most common reason practitioners get finished.

**Q2**: How should you position your chin and head to minimize choking pressure?
**A**: Tuck your chin tightly toward your free-side shoulder. This creates the smallest possible choking surface and protects your carotid arteries by positioning bony structures rather than soft tissue against the choking arm. Avoid turning your head away from opponent or lifting your chin — both actions expose more neck surface to the choke.

**Q3**: Your opponent begins stepping over your head to finish — what immediate action do you take?
**A**: Post your free hand on their hip or thigh immediately to prevent the step-over from completing. The step-over is the finishing sequence — once they complete it, escape becomes extremely difficult. Your free hand must leave the grip-fighting position and defensively post on their hip. Simultaneously, flatten your body and scoot hips away. If the step is mid-flight, roll in the same direction to deny them the dominant angle.

**Q4**: What is the correct hip movement when defending anaconda control?
**A**: Move your hips away from the opponent's chest pressure, not toward them. This reduces their ability to drive weight into your trapped shoulder and creates the angle needed to escape to guard. Coordinate hip movement with your upper body defense — as your free hand creates space at the neck, your hips move in the opposite direction. This combined movement works to open the constricting loop.

**Q5**: How do you manage your trapped arm to reduce choking effectiveness?
**A**: Keep your trapped arm bent with elbow close to your body rather than extended. An extended arm creates a larger loop for the opponent to constrict. By keeping the arm bent and pulled close, you minimize the space they can control. Additionally, rotate your trapped arm so your elbow points toward the ground — this reduces the mechanical advantage of the choke by changing the compression angle.

**Q6**: Your opponent initiates the roll-through finish — how do you counter?
**A**: If caught early, post your free hand on the mat and sprawl your legs back to resist the roll. If the roll is already committed, go with their momentum and immediately fight the grip on landing. Sometimes rolling with them and scrambling on landing is more effective than stopping a committed roll. Use the momentary chaos to attack their grip via **[[Transitions/Anaconda Grip Break to Front Headlock]]**.

**Q7**: What breathing strategy helps survive anaconda control?
**A**: Take short, controlled nasal breaths rather than gasping through your mouth. Deep panicked breaths expand your chest and tighten the choke mechanism. Stay calm and breathe shallowly while you work your defense. Relax muscles that aren't actively needed for escape — tension increases oxygen consumption. If you feel the choke getting tight and your defense isn't working, **tap early**. There is no shame in tapping to a properly locked anaconda.

**Q8**: When should you prioritize escaping versus tapping to the anaconda?
**A**: Tap when you feel tunnel vision starting, when you can no longer effectively fight the grip, or when the position is fully locked with no escape opportunity. The anaconda can cause unconsciousness rapidly once the blood choke is established. If your defensive hand fighting isn't creating space after 5–10 seconds of effort, the position is likely too deep to escape. In training, tap early — you gain nothing from going unconscious and lose the opportunity to drill the defense from earlier stages.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the anaconda choke in BJJ?

The **[[Transitions/Anaconda Choke]]** is a front headlock submission where the attacker threads their arm under the opponent's near-side arm and around their neck, locking a gable or figure-four grip. It is finished by rolling over the opponent's trapped shoulder. It is a blood choke that can cause unconsciousness in 8–12 seconds once fully locked.

### Is the anaconda choke a blood choke or air choke?

The anaconda is **primarily a blood choke** — it restricts blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries rather than blocking the airway. This is why it acts quickly and why significant pain often doesn't precede unconsciousness. **Tap early.** See the [Safety and Tap Protocol](#safety) section above.

### What is the difference between an anaconda choke and a D'arce choke?

Both are front headlock arm-in chokes, but arm position differs:
- **Anaconda**: attacking arm threads *under* your near arm and *around* your neck
- **[[Positions/Darce Control|D'arce (Brabo)]]**: attacking arm threads *over* your near arm and *under* your neck from the far side

The anaconda is typically finished by rolling; the D'arce can be finished statically with pressure. Both require immediate grip-breaking as the first defensive priority.

### How do you defend against the anaconda step-over finish?

Post your free hand on the attacker's hip or thigh the moment you see them beginning to step. The step-over is the final phase — prevent it before it starts. If the step is already happening, roll in the same direction as their momentum and use the scramble to attack the grip.

### What BJJ positions lead to the anaconda choke?

The anaconda is most commonly set up from [[Positions/Turtle]] (via [[Transitions/Anaconda from Turtle]]), failed takedown attempts, and [[Positions/Front Headlock]] scrambles. The key entry moment is when you lift your head or reach with your near arm from [[Positions/Turtle]], giving the top player the space to thread their arm under your arm and around your neck.

### How do you breathe while caught in a choke hold?

Short, controlled nasal breaths — never gasp. Expanding your chest tightens a constricting choke. Relax non-essential muscles to reduce oxygen demand. Work your defense systematically. If it's not working after 5–10 seconds, tap.

### Can you counter the anaconda roll-through?

Yes — if caught early in the roll, post on the mat and sprawl your legs back to resist. If the roll is already committed, go with their momentum and attack the grip on landing via **[[Transitions/Rolling Escape from Anaconda]]**. The scramble during the roll can create grip-breaking opportunities that static defense cannot.
