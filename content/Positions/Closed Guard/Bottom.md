<section id="overview" class="content-section">

**Closed Guard Bottom** is the foundational offensive guard in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu where you wrap your legs around your opponent's waist with ankles crossed behind their back, controlling them from underneath. Success rate: Retention 68%, Advancement 62%, Submission 42%. Recommended for all skill levels, from white belt through black belt.

Despite being the bottom player, closed guard grants significant control through a dual structure: locked legs restrict your opponent's hips while strategic grips on collar, sleeves, or wrists control their upper body. This combination lets you break posture, create angles, and threaten sweeps and submissions simultaneously. The **[[Positions/Closed Guard]]** position works equally well in gi, no-gi, self-defense, and MMA contexts, making it one of the most universally applicable positions in grappling.

Master practitioners treat closed guard as an active offensive platform, not a passive hold. Success requires constant hip movement to create angles, aggressive posture breaking to prevent defensive frames, and systematic combination attacks that create dilemmas — defending one technique opens another. Smaller practitioners regularly control and submit larger opponents from this position through superior leverage and timing.

</section>

<section id="state-invariants" class="content-section">

## What Is Closed Guard Bottom in BJJ?

**Closed Guard Bottom** is the position where you lie on your back with your legs locked around your opponent's waist, ankles crossed at their lower back. You are the bottom player, but you hold offensive control through leg pressure and upper-body grips.

- Your legs wrap completely around your opponent's waist with ankles crossed behind their back, creating a locked circuit that restricts their hip movement and prevents disengagement. Heels pull into their lower back to break posture while knees squeeze their ribs to restrict breathing and movement.
- Your back stays flat on the mat with shoulders in contact with the ground, hips elevated slightly to maintain connection and prevent being flattened. Head and upper back retain mobility to create angles and respond to pressure.
- Your opponent's torso is trapped between your legs with their hips unable to move laterally or create significant distance. Your hips stay connected to theirs, eliminating space that would allow them to establish posture or begin systematic passing sequences.
- Strategic grips on collar, sleeves, wrists, or behind the head control your opponent's upper body and prevent them from sitting back or creating defensive frames. Every grip serves both posture-breaking and offensive purposes.

**Closed Guard vs. Open Guard:** Closed guard has your ankles crossed behind the opponent's back, creating a locked control circuit. **[[Positions/Open Guard]]** keeps the legs uncrossed, using hooks, grips, or frames for control instead. Closed guard offers more defensive security and is more forgiving for beginners; open guard offers more mobility and variety of entries at the cost of easier guard-pass risk.

<section id="prerequisites" class="content-section">

## Prerequisites

- Proper back position with shoulders flat on mat and ability to maintain hip connection without being completely flattened by the opponent's weight
- Leg conditioning and hip flexibility for sustained guard closure with ability to cross ankles comfortably behind the opponent's back for extended periods
- Basic grip fighting skills to establish and maintain collar, sleeve, or wrist control against active resistance and grip-breaking attempts
- Understanding of posture-breaking mechanics using combined leg and arm pressure to pull the opponent forward and break their structural base
- Core strength to create angles by shifting hips laterally while maintaining guard closure and offensive pressure

<section id="key-principles" class="content-section">

## How to Play Closed Guard Bottom Effectively

- Maintain constant hip connection, eliminating space between your hips and your opponent's hips to prevent posture establishment and passing angles
- Control your opponent's posture through strategic grips on collar, sleeves, or behind the head, using combined arm and leg pressure to break them forward
- Create angles by shifting hips laterally 30-45 degrees to off-balance your opponent and expose their base, setting up sweeps and submissions from advantageous positions
- Threaten multiple attack vectors simultaneously, creating dilemmas where defending one attack necessarily opens another offensive pathway
- Manage defensive frames with arms to prevent your opponent from establishing heavy pressure while maintaining offensive grips that enable attacks
- Use active legs to pull your opponent forward with heels while squeezing knees together to restrict hip movement and breathing, creating cumulative fatigue

### Grip Strategy from Closed Guard

Every grip must serve an offensive purpose. Passive grips stall the match and favor the top player.

- **Collar grips** — break posture by pulling the opponent's head and shoulders forward. The foundation of closed guard control.
- **Sleeve grips** — control the arms for sweeps like the **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]** and **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]**. A sleeve grip on the far arm sets up the scissor; near arm for hip bump.
- **Wrist grips** — isolate the arms for submissions. The entry point for **[[Transitions/Triangle Setup]]**, **[[Transitions/Omoplata Sweep]]**, and the **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]**.
- **Behind-the-head grip** — most aggressive posture break. Pulls the opponent's forehead toward your chest, collapsing their frame entirely.

Transition between grip types actively to keep your opponent reacting. Static gripping eliminates your threat.

### Gi vs. No-Gi Closed Guard

**Gi:** Collar and sleeve grips give you durable handles that are difficult to break. The cross collar choke, triangle, and armbar are all high-percentage because grips hold through scrambles.

**No-Gi:** Grip targets shift to the head, underhooks, and wrist control. Collar and sleeve grips are unavailable, but the posture-breaking mechanics — heels in lower back, hips elevated, continuous angle creation — are identical. Submissions like the **[[Transitions/Triangle Choke]]**, armbar, guillotine, and rear naked choke remain highly effective from closed guard in no-gi.

## What to Do From Closed Guard Bottom (Decision Guide)

### How to Sweep from Closed Guard

Match your sweep to your opponent's reaction. Breaking posture and creating a 30-45 degree hip angle are prerequisites for every sweep below.

- **Opponent postures up (upright, hands on hips)** → **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** (60%) or **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]** (55%)
- **Opponent drives forward (shoulder pressure, trying to flatten you)** → **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]** (55%) or **[[Transitions/Flower Sweep]]** (50%)
- **Opponent posts hand on mat** → **[[Transitions/Kimura to Back Take]]** (55%) or **[[Transitions/Omoplata to Back]]** (45%)

<section id="common-mistakes" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Common Closed Guard Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Keeping flat, square position without creating angles or off-balancing opponent through hip movement

- ❌ **Consequence**: Makes it easy for opponent to maintain strong posture, reducing your ability to initiate effective attacks or sweeps. Static guard position allows opponent to dictate pace and work their passing game without pressure.
- ✅ **Correction**: Constantly shift hips laterally to create angles of 30-45 degrees, using off-balancing techniques to disrupt opponent's posture and open attack opportunities. Active hip movement creates dynamic threats that force defensive reactions.

### 2. Focusing on only one attack without chaining techniques or creating systematic dilemmas for opponent

- ❌ **Consequence**: Leads to predictable offense, allowing opponent to anticipate and counter your moves with prepared defenses. Single-minded attacks are easily defended by experienced practitioners who recognize setups.
- ✅ **Correction**: Develop combination attacks where defending one technique opens another. For example, **[[Transitions/Triangle Setup]]** attempt transitions to **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]**, or **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** to **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]**. Create constant dilemmas that force reactions opening new opportunities.

### 3. Allowing opponent to establish strong posture with arms extended and hips back creating distance

- ❌ **Consequence**: Eliminates offensive opportunities and puts you purely on defense. Strong posture allows opponent to begin guard-breaking sequences and passing attempts with minimal threat of counters or submissions.
- ✅ **Correction**: Immediately break posture by pulling collar down while driving heels into lower back. Use combined arm and leg pressure before opponent can establish solid base and structural integrity.

### 4. Crossing ankles too high on opponent's back or too low near hips compromising control quality

- ❌ **Consequence**: High ankle lock creates space at hips allowing guard break. Low ankle lock lacks control of upper body and allows opponent to posture easily. Both positions weaken overall guard effectiveness and offensive capability.
- ✅ **Correction**: Cross ankles at the small of opponent's lower back, just above their hips. This position maximizes control of both upper and lower body while maintaining sustainable squeeze that doesn't exhaust your legs prematurely.

### 5. Gripping without purpose or holding defensive grips that don't create offensive opportunities or advancement

- ❌ **Consequence**: Wastes energy on grips that don't advance position or create threats. Defensive gripping creates stalling position that favors the top player's passing game and initiative.
- ✅ **Correction**: Every grip should serve offensive purpose — collar grips break posture, sleeve grips control arms for sweeps, wrist grips set up submissions. Transition grips actively to create constant threats requiring defensive responses.

### 6. Letting legs become passive or losing squeeze pressure on opponent's torso during extended sequences

- ❌ **Consequence**: Allows opponent to create space, establish posture, and begin guard breaking. Passive legs signal defensive mindset and eliminate submission threats from guard while inviting passing pressure.
- ✅ **Correction**: Maintain active leg pressure with heels constantly pulling into lower back while knees squeeze ribs. Legs should be dynamic tools for control and off-balancing, not passive barriers waiting for opponent's initiative.

<section id="training-drills" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Closed Guard Drills and Training Exercises

### Posture Breaking Sequences

Practice posture-breaking sequences with progressive resistance, focusing on using collar grips combined with heel pressure to pull opponent forward. Partner starts with strong posture and gradually increases resistance as you develop timing and leverage mechanics. Work both gi collar grips and no-gi head control variations.

**Duration**: 5 minutes per round, 3-4 rounds


### Transition Chain Drilling

Drill transition chains between submissions and sweeps: **[[Transitions/Triangle Setup]]** to **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]** to **[[Transitions/Omoplata Sweep]]**, or **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** to **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]** to **[[Transitions/Flower Sweep]]**. Focus on fluid movement between techniques and maintaining control throughout transitions. Partner provides graduated resistance allowing technique refinement.

**Duration**: 10 minutes continuous flow


### Hip Mobility and Angle Creation

Work hip mobility drills specifically for closed guard, practicing shifting hips 45 degrees left and right while maintaining guard closure. Add resistance by having partner attempt to maintain square posture as you create angles. Develop the ability to create angles without breaking guard.

**Duration**: 5 minutes technique, 5 minutes live resistance


### Grip Fighting from Closed Guard

Specific training focused on establishing and maintaining dominant grips against an opponent actively fighting hands. Practice transitioning between collar, sleeve, and wrist grips while maintaining posture control. Work both offensive grip establishment and defensive grip breaking against partner's attempts.

**Duration**: 3 minute rounds, 4-5 rounds


### Closed Guard Retention Under Pressure

Partner attempts to break and pass closed guard using any legal methods while you maintain guard closure, break posture, and threaten sweeps. Builds endurance and defensive skills under realistic pressure. Focus on maintaining offensive mindset despite defensive pressure.

**Duration**: 5 minute rounds, 3-4 rounds

<section id="faq" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Frequently Asked Questions About Closed Guard

### What is closed guard in BJJ?

**Closed guard** is a ground position where the bottom player wraps their legs around the opponent's waist with ankles crossed behind their back. Despite being underneath, the guard player controls the pace through posture breaks, hip movement, and combination attacks. It is the most common guard position for beginners and remains effective at every skill level.

### What attacks can you do from closed guard?

From closed guard bottom you can execute sweeps (**[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** at 21% attempt rate, **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]**, **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]**, **[[Transitions/Flower Sweep]]**), submissions (**[[Transitions/Triangle Setup]]**, **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]**, **[[Transitions/Cross Collar Choke]]**, **[[Transitions/Omoplata Sweep]]**, **[[Transitions/Kimura to Back Take]]**), and back takes. The Hip Bump Sweep is the highest-probability option at 60% success when the opponent postures up.

### How do you sweep from closed guard?

Break posture first, then create a 30-45 degree hip angle. Match the sweep to your opponent's reaction: **upright posture** calls for **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** or **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]**; **forward pressure** calls for **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]** or **[[Transitions/Flower Sweep]]**; **posted hand** calls for **[[Transitions/Kimura to Back Take]]**. Every sweep becomes easier after posture is broken.

### How do you break posture in closed guard?

Pull your opponent's collar down with one or both hands while simultaneously driving your heels into their lower back. Use combined arm and leg pressure simultaneously — the collar pulls their head forward while heel pressure prevents them from sitting up. Without posture breaking, your opponent can simply wait out your guard and work systematic breaks.

### Is closed guard good for beginners in BJJ?

Yes — closed guard is the recommended starting guard for beginners. Its locked leg position provides defensive security that forgives errors which would cause guard loss in open guard systems. The structured offense (break posture → create angle → attack sweep or submission) gives beginners a clear framework. Most BJJ academies teach closed guard as the first guard system for these reasons.

### How do you keep your closed guard from being opened?

Cross ankles at the lower back (not too high, not too low), break posture immediately when the opponent tries to create distance, and stay active with grips and hip movement. Passive closed guard is the easiest to open because it creates no threat. Constant sweeping and submission attempts prevent the top player from setting up systematic guard breaks. Active guard is retained guard.

### What grips should you use in closed guard?

Every grip must serve an offensive purpose: **collar grips** break posture, **sleeve grips** set up sweeps like Scissor and Hip Bump, **wrist grips** isolate arms for submissions, and the **behind-the-head grip** is the most aggressive posture break. Avoid passive grips that stall without creating threats — they waste energy and cede initiative to the top player.

### Can you use closed guard in no-gi BJJ?

Yes. Closed guard works in no-gi, though grip targets shift from collar and sleeves to head control, underhooks, and wrist grips. Submissions like the triangle, armbar, guillotine, and rear naked choke remain highly effective. The core mechanics — heels in lower back, hip elevation, constant angle creation — are identical in gi and no-gi.

### What is the difference between closed guard and open guard?

Closed guard has ankles crossed behind the opponent's back, creating a locked control circuit. **[[Positions/Open Guard]]** keeps legs uncrossed, using hooks, grips, or frames for control. Closed guard offers more defensive security and is more forgiving for beginners; open guard offers more mobility and a wider variety of entries and attacks at the cost of greater guard-pass risk.

### How do you use closed guard in MMA?

In MMA, closed guard priorities shift toward preventing strikes, clinching to negate punching power, and securing high-percentage submissions like the armbar, triangle, and rear naked choke. Hip positioning and posture control remain critical — a broken posture eliminates the opponent's striking angles. Closed guard is one of the most common ground positions in MMA, particularly relevant for fighters who end up on the bottom after a takedown.
