<section id="overview" class="content-section">

**Closed Guard Bottom** is the foundational offensive guard position in BJJ where you control your opponent from underneath with legs locked around their waist, ankles crossed behind their back. Sweep success rates: **Hip Bump Sweep ~60%, Scissor Sweep ~55%, Pendulum Sweep ~55%**. Submission probability: 42%. Requires: posture breaking, hip mobility, active grips.

The power of closed guard bottom lies in its dual nature: it simultaneously provides defensive protection while enabling aggressive offense. Your locked legs create a closed kinetic chain controlling your opponent's hips, while your arms control their upper body through strategic grips on collar, sleeves, or wrists. This works equally well in gi, no-gi, and MMA contexts.

Master practitioners — including **Renzo Gracie**, **Marcelo Garcia**, and **Bernardo Faria** — treat closed guard as an active offensive platform, not a passive holding position. Success requires constant hip movement, aggressive posture breaking, and systematic combination attacks that create dilemmas where defending one technique opens another.

</section>

<section id="state-invariants" class="content-section">

## What Is Closed Guard Bottom in BJJ?

**[[Positions/Closed Guard]]** bottom occurs when:

- Your legs wrap completely around opponent's waist with ankles crossed behind their back, creating a locked circuit that restricts hip movement. Heels pull into lower back to break posture while knees squeeze ribs to restrict breathing.
- Your back remains flat on mat with shoulders in contact with ground, hips elevated slightly to maintain connection and prevent being flattened.
- Opponent's torso trapped between your legs with their hips unable to move laterally or create significant distance. Your hips stay connected to theirs, eliminating space for posture or passing sequences.
- Strategic grips established on collar, sleeves, wrists, or behind head to control opponent's upper body posture and prevent defensive frames.

**Is closed guard good for beginners?** Yes — it is the recommended starting guard for white belts because the locked legs provide security while you learn posture breaking and basic sweeps. The **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** and **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]** are among the first techniques taught in every BJJ curriculum.

## Prerequisites

- Proper back position with shoulders flat on mat and ability to maintain connection without being completely flattened
- Leg conditioning and hip flexibility for sustained guard closure with ankles crossed comfortably for extended periods
- Basic grip fighting skills to establish and maintain collar, sleeve, or wrist control against active resistance
- Understanding of posture breaking mechanics using combined leg and arm pressure
- Core strength to create angles by shifting hips laterally while maintaining guard closure

<section id="key-principles" class="content-section">

## How Do You Control the Opponent from Closed Guard Bottom?

- Maintain constant hip connection eliminating space between your hips and opponent's hips to prevent posture establishment and passing angles
- Control opponent's posture through strategic grips on collar, sleeves, or behind head using combined arm and leg pressure to pull them forward
- Create angles by shifting hips laterally to off-balance opponent and expose their base, setting up sweeps and submissions from advantageous positions
- Threaten multiple attack vectors simultaneously creating dilemmas where defending one attack necessarily opens another pathway
- Manage defensive frames with arms to prevent opponent from establishing heavy pressure while maintaining offensive grips
- Use active legs to pull opponent forward with heels while squeezing knees to restrict hip movement and breathing

<section id="common-mistakes" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Why Is Your Closed Guard Getting Passed? Common Mistakes

### 1. Keeping flat, square position without creating angles or off-balancing opponent through hip movement

- ❌ **Consequence**: Makes it easy for opponent to maintain strong posture, reducing your ability to initiate effective attacks or sweeps. Static guard position allows opponent to dictate pace.
- ✅ **Correction**: Constantly shift hips laterally to create angles of 30-45 degrees, using off-balancing techniques to disrupt opponent's posture. Active hip movement creates dynamic threats that force defensive reactions.

### 2. Focusing on only one attack without chaining techniques or creating systematic dilemmas

- ❌ **Consequence**: Leads to predictable offense, allowing opponent to anticipate and counter with prepared defenses.
- ✅ **Correction**: Develop combination attacks where defending one technique opens another. Triangle attempt → armbar, hip bump → pendulum sweep. Create constant dilemmas that force reactions opening new opportunities.

### 3. Allowing opponent to establish strong posture with arms extended and hips back creating distance

- ❌ **Consequence**: Eliminates offensive opportunities and puts you purely on defense. Strong posture allows guard breaking sequences with minimal threat of counters.
- ✅ **Correction**: Immediately break posture by pulling collar down while driving heels into lower back. Use combined arm and leg pressure before opponent can establish solid base.

### 4. Crossing ankles too high on opponent's back or too low near hips

- ❌ **Consequence**: High ankle lock creates space at hips allowing guard break. Low ankle lock lacks upper body control and allows opponent to posture easily.
- ✅ **Correction**: Cross ankles at the small of opponent's lower back, just above their hips. This maximizes control of both upper and lower body while maintaining sustainable squeeze.

### 5. Gripping without purpose or holding defensive grips that don't create offensive opportunities

- ❌ **Consequence**: Wastes energy on grips that don't advance position or create threats. Defensive gripping favors the top player's passing game.
- ✅ **Correction**: Every grip should serve offensive purpose — collar grips break posture, sleeve grips control arms for sweeps, wrist grips set up submissions. Transition grips actively to create constant threats.

### 6. Letting legs become passive or losing squeeze pressure during extended sequences

- ❌ **Consequence**: Allows opponent to create space, establish posture, and begin guard breaking. Passive legs eliminate submission threats while inviting passing pressure.
- ✅ **Correction**: Maintain active leg pressure with heels constantly pulling into lower back while knees squeeze ribs. Legs should be dynamic tools for control and off-balancing.



</section>

<section id="training-drills" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## How to Train Closed Guard Bottom: Drills That Build Real Skills

### Posture Breaking Sequences

Practice posture breaking with progressive resistance, focusing on collar grips combined with heel pressure to pull opponent forward. Partner starts with strong posture and gradually increases resistance. Work both gi collar grips and no-gi head control variations.

**Duration**: 5 minutes per round, 3-4 rounds


### Transition Chain Drilling

Drill transition chains between submissions and sweeps: **[[Transitions/Triangle Setup]]** → **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]** → omoplata, or **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** → pendulum → flower sweep. Focus on fluid movement between techniques while maintaining control throughout. Partner provides graduated resistance.

**Duration**: 10 minutes continuous flow


### Hip Mobility and Angle Creation

Work on hip mobility drills specifically for closed guard, practicing shifting hips 45 degrees left and right while maintaining guard closure. Add resistance by having partner attempt to maintain square posture as you create angles.

**Duration**: 5 minutes technique, 5 minutes live resistance


### Grip Fighting from Closed Guard

Specific training focused on establishing and maintaining dominant grips against an opponent actively fighting hands. Practice transitioning between collar, sleeve, and wrist grips while maintaining posture control.

**Duration**: 3 minute rounds, 4-5 rounds


### Closed Guard Retention Under Pressure

Partner attempts to break and pass closed guard using any legal methods while you work to maintain guard closure, break posture, and threaten sweeps. Builds endurance and defensive skills under realistic pressure. Focus on maintaining offensive mindset despite defensive pressure.

**Duration**: 5 minute rounds, 3-4 rounds

<section id="no-gi-adjustments" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## How Does Closed Guard Work in No-Gi BJJ?

Closed guard is effective in no-gi — adaptations are required for the grip game, not the core principles.

**Posture breaking without gi collar grips:**
- Overhook + head pull: wrap one arm over the opponent's shoulder and pull behind their head
- Wrist control: grab both wrists to limit their ability to post and frame
- Double underhooks around their torso for posture control

**Sweeps that work well in no-gi:**
- **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** — underhook drives apply directly without gi
- **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]** — grab wrist instead of sleeve
- **Scissor Sweep** — control wrist and elbow instead of sleeve and collar

**Submissions that transfer directly:**
- **[[Positions/Triangle Control]]** — triangle choke is gi-independent
- **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]** — arm mechanics unchanged
- Guillotine — open guard or closed guard entry
- Rear naked choke setup if sweep leads to **[[Positions/Back Control]]**

High-level no-gi competitors including Gordon Ryan and Craig Jones have built guard games incorporating closed guard fundamentals alongside modern leg lock entries.

</section>

<section id="guard-break-defense" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## How Do You Counter the Standing Guard Break?

When opponent stands to break your closed guard:

1. **Do not panic** — a standing opponent is vulnerable to sweeps
2. **Immediately threaten** with **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** or **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]** — the standing position exposes their balance
3. **Hip escape away** from their break direction to create angle and disrupt their base
4. **Attack actively** — a guard break requires them to posture, which opens sweep opportunities
5. **Transition proactively** to **[[Positions/Open Guard]]**, **[[Positions/Spider Guard]]**, or **[[Positions/Butterfly Guard]]** before the break is complete — do not hold closed guard passively waiting to lose it
6. **Control one leg** — grab their ankle or knee to create off-balance and sweep threat as they step back

The key principle: their standing guard break creates as much danger for them as for you, if you respond with immediate offensive threats rather than passive defense.

</section>

<section id="faq" class="content-section">

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is closed guard in BJJ?

**[[Positions/Closed Guard]]** is a fundamental BJJ position where the bottom player wraps their legs around the opponent's waist with ankles crossed behind the opponent's back, trapping them inside the guard. Despite being on the bottom, the guard player has significant control with access to sweeps, submissions (**[[Positions/Triangle Control]]**, **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]**, omoplata), and transitions. One of the most versatile positions from white belt through elite competition.

### What are the best sweeps from closed guard?

Most effective sweeps: **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** (~60% vs upright posture), **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]** (~55%), **[[Transitions/Pendulum Sweep]]** (~55% vs forward pressure), **[[Transitions/Flower Sweep]]** (~50%), and Kimura to Back Take (~55% when opponent posts hand). Chain sweeps to create dilemmas — use hip bump and pendulum as a two-option pair.

### What submissions can you do from closed guard?

Key submissions: **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]** (when opponent's arm is inside and posture is broken), Triangle Choke (when opponent places arm inside), Omoplata (from triangle setups), Cross Collar Choke (gi), and Guillotine (when opponent ducks their head). Submission threat is highest when posture is broken.

### How do you break posture in closed guard?

Break posture by simultaneously pulling down on the opponent's collar with both hands while driving your heels into their lower back. The combined arm-pull and heel-pressure creates a lever that disrupts their base. Cross ankles at the small of the back for maximum control. Once posture is broken, transition immediately to attacks — do not wait for them to re-establish base.

### Is closed guard good for beginners?

Yes — closed guard is the recommended starting guard for white belts. The locked legs provide physical security while you learn core principles: posture breaking, hip movement, and combination attacks. The **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** and **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]** are high-percentage moves that work at all levels. Closed guard rewards technique over strength, making it effective for smaller practitioners against larger opponents.

### What is the hip bump sweep?

The **[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]** is executed when the opponent maintains upright posture. The guard player sits up, establishes one underhook, and explosively drives their hip into the opponent's thigh while pushing with the underhook arm, toppling them to the side into **[[Positions/Mount]]**. Scores mount position (~60% success vs upright opponent). Combine with triangle or kimura to create a two-option dilemma.

### What is the scissor sweep?

The **[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]** is performed by creating a hip angle, establishing a collar and sleeve grip, then using one shin across the opponent's chest and the other shin against their knee — scissors motion — to off-balance them to the side, landing in **[[Positions/Mount]]**. Most effective against opponents who stay upright or lean forward without posting (~55% success).

### What is the difference between closed guard and open guard?

In **[[Positions/Closed Guard]]** the ankles are crossed behind the opponent's back, creating a locked, secure circuit with strong control but limited sweeping angles. In **[[Positions/Open Guard]]** the legs are not locked — the guard player uses various configurations (**[[Positions/Spider Guard]]**, De La Riva, **[[Positions/Butterfly Guard]]**, lasso) for dynamic sweeping and submission threats with more mobility. Closed guard excels for control and posture breaking; open guard excels for dynamic off-balancing.

### What grips should you use in closed guard?

**In gi:** collar grips (break posture), sleeve grips (control arms for sweeps and armbars), wrist grips (set up triangles and kimuras). **In no-gi:** overhook + head pull (posture break), wrist control (submission entries), underhook (hip bump and back takes). Every grip should serve an offensive purpose — avoid defensive grips that allow the top player to dictate pace and plan a guard break.

### Can you get submissions from closed guard against a stronger opponent?

Yes — closed guard is specifically designed to neutralize strength and size advantages through leverage. **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]** and triangle choke work through mechanical leverage that strength cannot override once properly locked. Breaking posture first is critical — a stronger opponent who maintains posture is harder to submit. Use leg and body weight (not arm strength) to break posture, then attack immediately before they re-establish base.
