<section id="overview" class="content-section">

The bottom player is responsible for maintaining the control. The bottom player faces the opponent and keeps the hips close beneath the shoulders. The legs form a high figure-four around the shoulder line, with both opposing arms inside. The top player has not cleared a shoulder or withdrawn the elbows below the controlling leg line. The hands can isolate an arm while the legs maintain the shoulder enclosure.

Bring the shoulders close before climbing the legs; reaching for a distant head leaves the guard loose. Confirm that both shoulders remain inside the high leg triangle rather than treating a one-arm triangle as Dead Orchard. Turn the hips and bring the knee toward the torso instead of hauling the foot across a fixed knee.

Hold an exposed arm without straightening it to its limit; leg control should carry the posture work. Adjust the hip angle if the opponent starts withdrawing a shoulder, and recover guard if the enclosure opens. Uncross the legs and let the arm go immediately on a tap, pain, or unsafe stacking pressure.

The defining feature is both shoulders and arms enclosed by the legs. An anaconda instead uses an arm loop around the neck and one arm from a front headlock; the two controls require different body configurations. Both arms may be trapped, so agree on verbal and foot taps. Never stack the neck, wrench a knee into the leg triangle, or test a captured elbow by pulling hard.

Keep a supported forearm inside if the top player begins driving chest pressure into you before both arms are captured. The high triangle controls the shoulders and arms; it does not replace the frames that keep a failed entry from becoming a pin.

</section>

## Position Definition

<p class="section-subtitle">What is Dead Orchard Control (Bottom)?</p>

- The bottom player faces the opponent and keeps the hips close beneath the shoulders.
- The legs form a high figure-four around the shoulder line, with both opposing arms inside.
- The top player has not cleared a shoulder or withdrawn the elbows below the controlling leg line.
- The hands can isolate an arm while the legs maintain the shoulder enclosure.

## Key Defensive Principles

<p class="section-subtitle">What are the key principles for defending Dead Orchard Control?</p>

- Break posture first: Bring the shoulders close before climbing the legs; reaching for a distant head leaves the guard loose.
- Enclose both arms: Confirm that both shoulders remain inside the high leg triangle rather than treating a one-arm triangle as Dead Orchard.
- Keep the knee supported: Turn the hips and bring the knee toward the torso instead of hauling the foot across a fixed knee.
- Control an elbow: Hold an exposed arm without straightening it to its limit; leg control should carry the posture work.
- Follow the shoulder line: Adjust the hip angle if the opponent starts withdrawing a shoulder, and recover guard if the enclosure opens.
- Release before loading: Uncross the legs and let the arm go immediately on a tap, pain, or unsafe stacking pressure.


</section>
