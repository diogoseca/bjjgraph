**Aoki Lock Bottom** is a critical defensive scenario in BJJ where you are being attacked with a leg-entangled shoulder lock. Escape rate: 50% overall. Named after Japanese submission grappler **Shinya Aoki**, this position occurs when your opponent has secured leg entanglement control over your shoulder while applying rotational hip pressure — typically from **[[Positions/Side Control]]**, **[[Positions/Turtle]]**, or transitional scrambles.

Unlike a **[[Positions/Kimura Trap]]** or traditional **[[Positions/Omoplata Control]]**, the Aoki Lock combines leg trapping with shoulder manipulation. The leg entanglement limits your natural escape routes, preventing the rolling defense that works against most shoulder locks. Proper technique can still turn this position into a pathway back to **[[Positions/Closed Guard]]** or even a position reversal — but only through specific movement patterns that address both the shoulder pressure and the leg control simultaneously.

## What Is the Aoki Lock Bottom Position?

**Aoki Lock Bottom** is the position where your opponent has trapped your arm with a leg entanglement — thigh over your shoulder, shin over your upper arm — and is applying hip-driven rotational pressure to your shoulder joint. You are on your side or stomach with limited rotation capability.

- Your shoulder is trapped by your opponent's leg entanglement with thigh over shoulder and shin controlling upper arm
- Your opponent maintains hip pressure directed into your shoulder joint creating rotational stress on the shoulder capsule
- Your trapped arm is extended and isolated between your opponent's legs with limited rotation capability
- Your opponent posts their free leg on the mat for stable base while hands control your hips to prevent escape angles
- Your defensive posture focuses on preventing full shoulder rotation while creating space for arm extraction

### About the Aoki Lock: Who Is Shinya Aoki?

The **Aoki Lock** is named after **Shinya Aoki**, a Japanese submission grappler and MMA fighter renowned for his unorthodox, flexible guard game. Aoki popularized this leg-entangled shoulder lock in MMA competition during the late 2000s and early 2010s, finishing multiple opponents with it from unusual scramble positions. His signature flexible style allowed him to enter the lock from positions where traditional shoulder locks were unavailable — side control transitions, turtle attacks, and live scrambles. The lock bears his name because he made it a reliable competition weapon rather than a curiosity.

### How the Aoki Lock Works Mechanically

The Aoki Lock works by trapping your arm with a leg entanglement — thigh over your shoulder, shin over your upper arm — and then driving the hips toward your back to create internal-to-external rotational stress on the shoulder capsule.

The critical difference from other shoulder locks: the leg trap prevents you from rolling your body to neutralize pressure. In a standard **[[Transitions/Omoplata Sweep]]**, the bottom player can roll forward to escape. In the Aoki Lock, the leg entanglement blocks this roll, so pressure accumulates faster and escapes must work around the leg control rather than through it.

### Aoki Lock vs. Omoplata vs. Kimura

| Lock | Control Method | Entry | Key Weakness |
|------|---------------|-------|--------------|
| **Aoki Lock** | Leg entanglement (thigh over shoulder, shin on upper arm) | Side control, turtle, scrambles | Requires addressing leg trap before arm extraction |
| **[[Transitions/Omoplata Sweep\|Omoplata]]** | Figure-four leg lock from guard | Closed guard, spider guard | Bottom player can roll forward to escape |
| **[[Positions/Kimura Trap\|Kimura]]** | Figure-four arm grip (wrist + elbow) | Half guard, side control, guard | Grip-fight can break the control |

The Aoki Lock is harder to escape than a kimura because there is no grip to peel — the leg entanglement cannot be grip-fought away. It is harder to escape than an omoplata because the leg trap prevents the standard forward roll defense.

## How to Survive and Escape the Aoki Lock

- Keep your shoulder internally rotated to resist external rotation pressure from your opponent's hip drive — this is your primary injury-prevention mechanism
- Prevent full arm extension by keeping your elbow bent when possible without compromising shoulder safety
- Use your free hand to attack your opponent's posted leg base or hip control to create escape angles — this is your primary offensive tool
- **Roll toward your trapped arm**, following the path of least resistance rather than fighting against pressure. Rolling away dramatically increases shoulder torque.
- Create hip movement to reduce the pressure angle and generate escape momentum before the lock fully tightens
- Recognize your tap threshold early — shoulder locks cause serious injury if defended too aggressively. **Tap early.**
- Prioritize position escape over submission defense if pressure becomes dangerous

## Aoki Lock Defense Mistakes That Cause Injury

### 1. Rolling away from trapped arm direction against the pressure

- ❌ **Consequence**: Increases shoulder torque dramatically, often leading to submission or serious shoulder injury. This is the most dangerous mistake you can make — it actively works against you.
- ✅ **Correction**: Roll toward your trapped arm, following the path of least resistance and using your opponent's control momentum. The direction feels counterintuitive but is the correct escape vector.

### 2. Attempting to muscle arm free without addressing leg entanglement

- ❌ **Consequence**: Exhausts energy while increasing shoulder stress. Makes escape impossible while risking injury. The arm cannot be extracted while the leg trap is intact.
- ✅ **Correction**: Address leg control first by creating hip angles or attacking your opponent's base before attempting arm extraction. Loosen the leg trap, then extract the arm.

### 3. Remaining static and defensive without active escape attempts

- ❌ **Consequence**: Allows your opponent time to perfect the pressure angle and finish the submission safely. Static defense lets them tighten control without opposition.
- ✅ **Correction**: Move immediately upon recognizing the position. Create continuous movement toward escape rather than static defense. The window for escape narrows as the lock tightens.

### 4. Defending beyond safe shoulder range to avoid tapping

- ❌ **Consequence**: Serious shoulder injury including rotator cuff tears, labrum damage, or shoulder dislocation. These injuries can require surgery and months of recovery.
- ✅ **Correction**: Tap early when shoulder pressure reaches discomfort threshold. The position is not worth injury risk. Tap, reset, and develop the escape in controlled drilling.

### 5. Neglecting to use free hand for base attacks or hip control

- ❌ **Consequence**: Wastes your primary offensive tool for creating escape opportunities and destabilizing your opponent's control. A free hand doing nothing is wasted leverage.
- ✅ **Correction**: Immediately engage your free hand in attacking your opponent's posted leg or controlling their hip to create angles. This is your best tool for disrupting the lock before it fully tightens.

**Duration**: 5 minutes per side

## Aoki Lock Defense Q&A

**Q1**: Your opponent increases hip pressure on your shoulder — what immediate defensive adjustment should you make?
**A**: Internally rotate your shoulder to resist the external rotation pressure from their hip drive. Simultaneously initiate a roll toward your trapped arm direction, using their forward pressure momentum to assist your escape. Do not fight the pressure by pulling away — this dramatically increases shoulder torque and injury risk.

**Q2**: What are the essential defensive priorities when caught in Aoki Lock bottom position?
**A**: First priority is protecting your shoulder from catastrophic rotation by keeping it internally rotated. Second is addressing the leg entanglement before attempting arm extraction. Third is using your free hand to attack your opponent's posted leg base or control their hip. Fourth is creating continuous movement toward escape rather than static defense.

**Q3**: How do you shut down your opponent's ability to finish the submission?
**A**: Keep your trapped elbow bent when possible without compromising shoulder safety to prevent full arm extension. Roll toward the trapped arm direction following the path of least resistance. Attack your opponent's posted leg to destabilize their base and pressure generation. Create hip movement to reduce the pressure angle. Most importantly, tap early if pressure reaches a dangerous threshold.

**Q4**: What grip or frame priorities help you survive and escape Aoki Lock bottom?
**A**: Your free hand should immediately engage in attacking your opponent's posted leg or controlling their hip to create escape angles. Do not grip your trapped arm trying to pull it free — this wastes energy and doesn't address the leg entanglement. Focus frames on creating space and destabilizing your opponent's base rather than directly fighting the submission.

**Q5**: How do you recognize when you should tap rather than continue defending?
**A**: Tap immediately when you feel sharp pain in the shoulder joint, when you cannot prevent your arm from full extension, when shoulder rotation reaches uncomfortable levels, or when your opponent's pressure is increasing and you have no escape path. Shoulder injuries from this position include rotator cuff tears, labrum damage, and dislocation — none are worth continuing defense.

**Q6**: Your opponent adjusts their posted leg position — how do you capitalize on this moment?
**A**: Attack their base immediately with your free hand before they re-establish stable posting. Time your arm extraction attempt to coincide with their adjustment, since leg entanglement often loosens during repositioning. Use their momentary instability to create hip movement and change your angle toward escape. This is your primary window for successful escape.

**Q7**: How do you manage energy when defending Aoki Lock bottom position?
**A**: Avoid fighting the submission with constant maximum tension — this exhausts you quickly. Focus on efficient movement patterns rather than strength-based resistance. Time your escape attempts to coincide with your opponent's adjustments or pressure changes. Accept that some pressure is inevitable and conserve energy for explosive escape attempts at opportune moments.

**Q8**: Your escape attempt fails but you've created some space — how do you prevent your opponent from fully re-establishing control?
**A**: Keep moving immediately rather than pausing in the partial escape position. If you've loosened the leg entanglement, continue working arm extraction before they re-tighten. If you've disrupted their base, continue attacking it to prevent re-stabilization. Chain your escape attempts together rather than treating each as isolated. Continuous movement is more effective than single powerful attempts.

## Frequently Asked Questions About the Aoki Lock

### What is the Aoki Lock in BJJ?

The **Aoki Lock** is a leg-entangled shoulder lock named after Japanese submission grappler Shinya Aoki. The attacker traps the opponent's arm with a leg entanglement — thigh over the shoulder, shin controlling the upper arm — then drives their hips into the shoulder to create rotational stress on the shoulder capsule. It is distinct from the **[[Transitions/Omoplata Sweep|omoplata]]** and **[[Positions/Kimura Trap|kimura]]** in that the control is entirely leg-based, making it harder to grip-fight out of.

### Who invented the Aoki Lock?

The Aoki Lock is named after **Shinya Aoki**, a Japanese submission grappler and MMA fighter who popularized this leg-entangled shoulder lock in competition during the late 2000s. Aoki is known for his unorthodox, flexible guard game and his use of unusual submission entries from scramble positions. He made the technique a reliable competition weapon, which is why it bears his name.

### How do you escape an Aoki Lock?

The primary escape is the **[[Transitions/Rolling Escape to Guard]]** (55% success rate): roll toward your trapped arm — never away from it — while attacking your opponent's posted leg with your free hand. Address the leg entanglement before attempting arm extraction. The **[[Transitions/Arm Extraction to Turtle]]** (38% attempt rate) and **[[Transitions/Granby Roll to Guard]]** (18% attempt rate) are secondary options depending on how the control is applied.

### Is the Aoki Lock dangerous?

Yes. The Aoki Lock is highly dangerous because the leg entanglement limits your ability to roll to safety, causing pressure to build faster than in most shoulder locks. Potential injuries include rotator cuff tears, labrum damage, and shoulder dislocation. **Tap early** — at the onset of rotational pressure, not at the edge of injury.

### What is the difference between an Aoki Lock and an omoplata?

Both use leg entanglement to attack the shoulder, but from different positions and with different mechanics. The omoplata traps the arm with a figure-four leg configuration from guard, and the bottom player can often roll forward to escape. The Aoki Lock uses leg entanglement from side control, turtle, or scrambles — the leg trap prevents the standard forward roll escape, making it harder to defend.

### What is the difference between an Aoki Lock and a kimura?

A **[[Positions/Kimura Trap|kimura]]** attacks the shoulder through a figure-four grip on the wrist and elbow, rotating the arm behind the back. The Aoki Lock uses leg entanglement instead of an arm grip — you cannot grip-fight your way out of a leg trap. This makes the Aoki Lock harder to defend against actively fighting opponents because there is no grip to peel.

### How do you defend against an Aoki Lock?

Keep your trapped shoulder internally rotated to resist external rotation pressure. Roll toward — not away from — your trapped arm. Engage your free hand to attack your opponent's base or hip. Move continuously rather than staying static. Tap early when shoulder pressure reaches a dangerous threshold to prevent injury.

### What BJJ positions lead to an Aoki Lock?

The Aoki Lock typically arises from **[[Positions/Turtle]]** position, guard-passing scrambles, or arm isolation during transitions from **[[Positions/Side Control]]**. Attackers most commonly enter by threading a leg over the shoulder during side control transitions, or from the turtle when your arm is isolated. Understanding these entries helps you recognize the position developing before it is fully locked in.
