<section id="overview" class="content-section">

**Aoki Lock Top** is an advanced leg-entangled shoulder lock control position where the top practitioner traps the opponent's arm between their legs and drives hip pressure into the shoulder joint. Named after **Shinya Aoki**, Japan's premier no-gi submission specialist, it combines omoplata-style mechanics with top-side control and delivers a **68% finish rate** with a **78% retention rate**. It most commonly arises from attacking a [[Positions/Turtle|turtled]] or prone opponent from [[Positions/Front Headlock|front headlock]] — and serves as a hub for finishes, [[Positions/Back Control|back takes]], and [[Positions/Truck|truck system]] entries.

</section>

<section id="state-invariants" class="content-section">

## What Is the Aoki Lock Top Position in BJJ?

- Top player's legs entangle the opponent's trapped arm: thigh positioned over shoulder, shin controlling the upper arm
- Opponent is typically on their side or stomach with the trapped shoulder isolated and unable to rotate freely
- Top player maintains base through a free leg posted on the mat, creating a stable platform for shoulder pressure
- Hip pressure is directed downward and forward onto the opponent's shoulder joint, creating rotational stress on the shoulder capsule
- Opponent's trapped arm is extended and isolated between the top player's legs, with the elbow pointing away from their body
- Top player's hands control the opponent's hips or far-side limbs, preventing rotation and escape angles

</section>

<section id="origin" class="content-section">

## Who Invented the Aoki Lock?

The Aoki Lock is named after **Shinya Aoki**, a Japanese professional MMA fighter and submission grappler who rose to prominence in the mid-2000s. Aoki became known for unorthodox flexibility-based attacks — including leg-entangled shoulder locks applied from top position — that defied conventional grappling logic. His consistent use of this position in MMA and submission wrestling competition gave the technique its name and cemented its place in the modern no-gi arsenal.

</section>

<section id="prerequisites" class="content-section">

## Prerequisites

- Successful entry from [[Positions/Front Headlock|front headlock]], [[Positions/Turtle|turtle]], or guard passing sequence with the opponent's arm isolated
- Opponent's shoulder controlled with legs positioned for entanglement
- Limited rotation capability in the opponent's trapped shoulder
- Base established through posted leg and hip positioning
- Understanding of shoulder lock mechanics and progressive pressure application protocols

</section>

<section id="entry" class="content-section">

## How to Enter the Aoki Lock from Turtle (Step-by-Step)

Follow these 8 steps to establish Aoki Lock from the [[Positions/Turtle|turtle position]]:

1. **Circle to the Side**: From [[Positions/Front Headlock|front headlock]] or turtle control, circle to the side of the arm you intend to trap.
2. **Isolate the Arm**: Thread your near hand under the opponent's armpit and grip their wrist or forearm to prevent elbow tucking.
3. **Swing Near Leg Over**: Swing your near leg over the opponent's trapped arm, placing your thigh across their shoulder. Your shin should pin their upper arm.
4. **Hook Far Leg Behind Shoulder**: Hook your far leg behind the opponent's shoulder or under their armpit to complete the leg entanglement.
5. **Post Your Free Foot**: Plant your free foot flat on the mat beside the opponent's hip to establish base and block forward rolls.
6. **Control the Hips**: Place both hands on the opponent's far hip to prevent rotation away from your shoulder pressure.
7. **Extend the Trapped Arm**: Tighten the leg entanglement to force the opponent's elbow away from their body — the arm must be fully straightened.
8. **Apply Hip Pressure Gradually**: Drive your hips downward and forward into the shoulder joint over 3-5 seconds minimum, monitoring for tap signals at all times.

</section>

<section id="key-principles" class="content-section">

## How Do You Control the Aoki Lock from the Top?

- Maintain constant hip pressure directed into the opponent's shoulder to prevent rotation and sustain the submission threat
- Control the opponent's trapped arm angle with your legs, keeping the elbow pointed away from their body
- Post the free leg strongly to create stable base and prevent the opponent from rolling through the position
- Apply pressure gradually and progressively over 3-5 seconds minimum — monitor tap signals continuously
- Control the opponent's hips with your hands to prevent them from turning into or away from the pressure
- Keep weight distributed through hips into the shoulder rather than relying on arm strength alone
- Maintain leg entanglement tightness to prevent arm extraction and position escape

</section>

<section id="vs-omoplata" class="content-section">

## Aoki Lock vs. Omoplata: What Is the Difference?

Both the **Aoki Lock** and the **[[Positions/Omoplata Control|Omoplata]]** are leg-entangled shoulder locks, but they differ fundamentally in position and shoulder mechanics:

| | Aoki Lock | Omoplata |
|---|---|---|
| **Attacker position** | On top (opponent turtled or prone) | On bottom (from guard) |
| **Shoulder rotation** | External rotation via downward hip pressure | Internal rotation via outward hip pressure |
| **Key entries** | Turtle, Front Headlock | Closed Guard, Spider Guard |
| **Primary transitions** | Back Control, Truck system | Sweep, Back take |
| **Competition level** | Advanced (restricted at lower IBJJF levels) | All levels |

The Aoki Lock's top-side leverage typically allows for stronger hip pressure and more direct transitions to [[Positions/Back Control|back control]] and the [[Positions/Truck|truck system]], making it a preferred weapon for no-gi specialists.

</section>

<section id="competition" class="content-section">

## Is the Aoki Lock Legal in BJJ Competition?

Legality depends on the ruleset and belt level:

- **ADCC / Submission-only (EBI rules)**: Legal at all levels — shoulder locks are unrestricted.
- **IBJJF No-Gi**: Legal at brown and black belt levels; restricted at lower belt divisions under shoulder lock classification rules.
- **IBJJF Gi**: Treated as an omoplata-category shoulder lock; restricted at white and blue belt levels.
- **Local competitions**: Rules vary — always confirm with event organizers before competing.

Regardless of ruleset, always apply the Aoki Lock gradually and release immediately upon any tap signal. Shoulder locks carry significant injury risk when applied explosively.

</section>

<section id="common-mistakes" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Why Does the Aoki Lock Fail? (Common Mistakes and Corrections)

### 1. Posting too far from opponent with free leg reducing hip pressure on shoulder

- ❌ **Consequence**: Opponent gains space to rotate shoulder and escape the lock or extract their trapped arm
- ✅ **Correction**: Keep posted leg close to opponent's body maintaining constant downward hip pressure into shoulder joint

### 2. Allowing opponent's elbow to bend and come back toward their body

- ❌ **Consequence**: Shoulder lock loses effectiveness as joint alignment changes allowing opponent to defend or escape
- ✅ **Correction**: Use leg positioning to keep opponent's arm extended with elbow pointing away from their torso

### 3. Failing to control opponent's hips with hands

- ❌ **Consequence**: Opponent can turn into pressure or create angles for escape negating the shoulder lock
- ✅ **Correction**: Maintain active hand control on opponent's far hip or pants to prevent rotation and movement

### 4. Applying shoulder pressure too quickly or explosively without progressive build

- ❌ **Consequence**: Risk of serious shoulder injury to opponent, potential disqualification, and unsafe training environment
- ✅ **Correction**: Apply pressure gradually over 3-5 seconds minimum staying alert for tap signals prioritizing safety

### 5. Losing leg entanglement tension around opponent's arm

- ❌ **Consequence**: Opponent can extract their arm and escape to guard or turtle position
- ✅ **Correction**: Maintain constant squeeze with legs around opponent's shoulder and upper arm complex

### 6. Looking away from opponent or losing positional awareness

- ❌ **Consequence**: Miss defensive movements, fail to see tap signals, or get swept to inferior position
- ✅ **Correction**: Keep head position aware with eyes on opponent's body language and hand signals throughout

### 7. Forcing finish when transitions are available

- ❌ **Consequence**: Waste energy on low-percentage finish while missing high-percentage transition opportunities
- ✅ **Correction**: Recognize when opponent's defense is strong and transition to back control, truck, or crucifix

</section>

Partner provides 50% resistance while you maintain Aoki Lock top position. They attempt common escapes (roll through, arm extraction, hip escape) while you maintain control and counter. Focus on weight distribution, base posting, and hip pressure. 3-minute rounds.

**Duration**: 15 minutes (5 rounds)


### Transition Flow Drill

From Aoki Lock top position, partner gives specific defensive reactions (rolling, turning away, exposing far arm). **Duration**: 12 minutes


### Safety-First Finish Practice

With a compliant partner, practice applying Aoki Lock pressure using 3-5 second gradual increases. Partner taps at first discomfort. Focus on immediate release protocol and communication. Develop sensitivity to submission depth and tap recognition. 8-10 controlled repetitions per side.

**Duration**: 10 minutes

## Test Your Knowledge

**Q1**: Your opponent starts bridging explosively while you have Aoki Lock control — what adjustment prevents escape?
**A**: Drive your hips forward and down into their shoulder while simultaneously tightening your leg triangle around their arm. Keep your posted leg close to their body to maintain base against the bridge. If they generate significant momentum, follow their movement and transition to back control rather than fighting the bridge directly.

**Q2**: What are the essential control points for maintaining Aoki Lock top position?
**A**: Three primary control points must be maintained simultaneously: leg entanglement around the opponent's arm and shoulder complex, hip pressure directed into the shoulder joint creating rotational stress, and hand control on opponent's hips or far-side limbs preventing rotation. The posted leg provides base stability. Losing any single control point significantly compromises position effectiveness.

**Q3**: How do you shut down the opponent's primary escape when they try to roll forward through the position?
**A**: Anticipate the roll by keeping your weight distribution forward through your hips rather than sitting back. As they initiate the roll, follow their momentum while maintaining leg entanglement — this often converts directly to back control. Do not fight the roll by pulling backward, as this creates space for arm extraction.

**Q4**: What grip priorities should you establish when entering Aoki Lock top position?
**A**: Primary grip is controlling opponent's far hip with your hands to prevent rotation and escape angles. Secondary priority is managing their free arm to prevent posting or defensive frames. Your legs provide the primary shoulder control through entanglement, so hand grips focus on hip and position management rather than the trapped arm itself.

**Q5**: How do you apply progressive pressure for the submission without risking injury to your training partner?
**A**: Apply shoulder pressure gradually over 3-5 seconds minimum using controlled hip drive rather than explosive movement. Maintain constant visual awareness of your partner's tap signals including verbal taps, hand taps on any surface, and body language indicating distress. Release immediately upon any tap signal.

**Q6**: Your opponent is defending well with their shoulder positioned to resist pressure — when should you transition rather than force the finish?
**A**: Transition when you've applied two attempts at increasing pressure without submission progress, when opponent's defensive posture is strong and static, or when they create any opening for back control, truck, or crucifix. Forcing a defended shoulder lock wastes energy. Recognizing transition opportunities converts positional control into advancement.

**Q7**: How do you manage energy expenditure when holding Aoki Lock top position?
**A**: Use body weight through hips rather than muscular effort for shoulder pressure. Keep legs actively squeezing but avoid maximum tension constantly. Post your free leg strongly but efficiently without over-extension. Let gravity and positioning do the work rather than fighting against opponent's defensive tension. The position should feel sustainable for 60-90 seconds.

**Q8**: Your opponent partially extracts their arm but you still have leg contact — how do you recover full control?
**A**: Immediately re-tighten your leg triangle before they complete extraction by driving your top leg down while pulling your bottom leg up. Simultaneously drive hip pressure forward to prevent further extraction. If extraction continues, abandon the shoulder lock and transition to back control or turtle attacks using your existing leg positioning as a starting point.

<section id="faq" class="content-section">

## Frequently Asked Questions About the Aoki Lock

### What is the Aoki Lock in BJJ?

The **Aoki Lock** is a leg-entangled shoulder lock named after Japanese submission grappler Shinya Aoki. The attacker traps the opponent's arm between their legs and drives their hips into the shoulder joint, creating external rotational stress that can force a tap. It commonly arises from the [[Positions/Turtle|turtle position]] and [[Positions/Front Headlock|front headlock]] in no-gi grappling and serves as a hub for [[Positions/Back Control|back takes]] and [[Positions/Truck|truck system]] entries.

### Who invented the Aoki Lock?

The technique is named after **Shinya Aoki**, a Japanese professional MMA fighter and submission grappler who popularized leg-entangled shoulder attacks during the mid-2000s. Aoki's unorthodox flexibility-based game made this position famous in MMA and submission wrestling competition, giving it his name.

### How do you escape the Aoki Lock?

Primary escapes: (1) **Roll forward** through the lock — this releases shoulder pressure but concedes [[Positions/Back Control|back control]]; (2) **Tuck the elbow** back to the body before the lock is fully established; (3) **Create hip distance** to relieve pressure and recompose guard. Escaping after the lock is tight is very difficult — early recognition and proactive defense are essential.

### What positions can you transition to from Aoki Lock top?

Key transitions include [[Transitions/Aoki Lock to Back Control|Back Control]] (most common when opponent rolls forward), the [[Positions/Truck|Truck system]] via [[Transitions/Truck Entry|Truck Entry]] (when opponent turns away), [[Transitions/Aoki Lock to Crucifix|Crucifix]] (when far arm is exposed), and [[Positions/Kimura Trap|Kimura Trap]] via [[Transitions/Kimura Switch|Kimura Switch]] (when a kimura grip becomes available during defense).

### Is the Aoki Lock legal in BJJ competitions?

Legality varies: legal in ADCC and submission-only events, restricted at lower belt levels in IBJJF due to shoulder lock classification rules. Always verify competition rules before using this technique and always apply pressure gradually in training — release immediately on any tap signal.

### How do you apply the Aoki Lock safely?

Apply shoulder pressure gradually over 3-5 seconds minimum rather than explosively. Maintain eye contact with your partner for tap signals — accept verbal, hand, and body-language taps and release immediately. The shoulder joint is vulnerable to fast application. Prioritize your partner's safety, especially with unfamiliar training partners.
