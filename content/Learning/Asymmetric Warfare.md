<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Choose exchanges where your practiced skills fit the position instead of assuming you must match an opponent at their preferred game.</p>
<p>A partner may repeatedly win one kind of exchange without controlling every other option. Look for a change you can make in this round: the distance you engage from, the side you pass toward, or the guard connection you offer.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Choose a concrete adjustment to the matchup: passing side, engagement distance, or the position you work toward.</li><li>Include the cost of the change. Avoiding one difficult exchange is a poor bargain if the new entry immediately gives away control.</li><li>Treat an opponent’s apparent weakness as a hypothesis; check it across more than one exchange.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>Use body size, stance, and pace as information, not as proof of someone’s strengths or limitations.</li><li>In practice, also take rounds outside your preferred game so this strategy does not become avoidance.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Your partner repeatedly off-balances you from their established closed guard</h3><p>Prioritize posture and a practiced guard-opening sequence. Once the guard opens, use a passing route you know rather than volunteering the same closed-guard exchange.</p><p class="learning-result">You change the problem while still respecting the controls already in place.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Your half guard entries keep meeting a crossface before you can turn onto your side</h3><p>Try managing distance in an open guard before the passer closes in, or rebuild a knee shield before seeking your underhook. Check whether the change lets you work; do not keep offering the same flattened entry simply because half guard is your preferred position.</p><p class="learning-result">The adjustment addresses the control this partner keeps winning.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>A partner recovers well on one side during passing rounds</h3><p>Test the other side with a real passing attempt and compare the result. Keep your base and grips; a direction change is not a reason to abandon control.</p><p class="learning-result">You gather useful evidence instead of building a strategy around one lucky pass.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Deciding what someone cannot do from their size or belt</h3><p>Your plan ignores the skills they actually show.</p><p>Update your read from their balance, grips, recoveries, and responses in the round.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Avoiding every position outside your strongest game</h3><p>Practice gives you little chance to improve the places where you get stuck.</p><p>Separate rounds for testing your main game from rounds that start in a weak position.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Change the starting distance</h3><p>Start seated against a standing passer. First try to establish your usual close connection; in the next exchange, keep a foot or shin frame managing distance before seeking grips. Top tries to pass both times. Reset on a pass, sweep, or tap and compare which entry preserved your posture.</p><p class="learning-focus">Test whether a different engagement changes the problem this partner presents.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more exercise</summary><div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Test the alternative</h3><p>Repeat a passing round from the same open guard, first using your usual side and then the other. Reset after each pass, sweep, or tap. Compare the grips and reactions you encountered, not just which round you won.</p><p class="learning-focus">Base a tactical choice on an observable difference.</p></div></details>

</section>
