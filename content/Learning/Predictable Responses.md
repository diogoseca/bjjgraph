<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Useful follow-ups begin with the response you can observe, not the reaction you expected an opponent to give.</p>
<p>A familiar attack often draws familiar defenses, but people can post, retreat, counter, or do nothing. Start with two likely responses and identify the body position that makes each follow-up available.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Describe a response as a visible change: a hand posts, weight moves back, or a knee comes inside.</li><li>Treat common reactions as possibilities, not hardwired rules.</li><li>Keep using the first attack when the opponent has not stopped it.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>If the expected cue is absent, keep control or rebuild instead of launching the memorized answer.</li><li>Review the response itself after a round; a failed follow-up may reflect a wrong read rather than a bad technique.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Your butterfly sweep makes the opponent post a hand</h3><p>Check whether that hand remains available and whether you still control their upper body. Choose a continuation suited to those connections rather than reaching for the arm after it has already returned.</p><p class="learning-result">The follow-up is tied to a present opening.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>You pull forward from closed guard and the opponent regains upright posture</h3><p>If their weight moves back and you can sit up with control, a hip-bump entry may be available. If they stay close, use the connection you have instead of forcing the sit-up.</p><p class="learning-result">The same initial action gives information for two different decisions.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>A guard player turns their knees toward your passing direction</h3><p>Watch whether their hips follow and which knee can still re-enter. Change direction only while your grips and balance allow it.</p><p class="learning-result">You respond to the defensive shape, not simply to seeing movement.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Believing a push always creates a push back</h3><p>Some partners retreat, change angle, or attack instead.</p><p>Test the response at manageable intensity and prepare for more than one result.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Remembering the technique name but missing the cue</h3><p>You attempt a valid follow-up in the wrong position.</p><p>State the required hand, hip, or weight change before drilling the continuation.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Two responses, unscripted</h3><p>Choose a familiar guard attack. Agree on two realistic defenses, then let top choose either without announcing it. Bottom follows the actual cue; reset on a sweep, pass, lost connection, or tap.</p><p class="learning-focus">Separate recognizing the defense from memorizing a sequence.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more exercise</summary><div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Add the missing answer</h3><p>Repeat the same exercise with the partner also allowed to leave the first attack available. Finish that attack when possible; stop and reset at the result.</p><p class="learning-focus">Avoid switching merely because you expect a defense.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>Your planned follow-up needs a posted hand, but the hand is already back. What now?</summary><p>Do not chase the absent opening. Continue an available attack, retain your position, or rebuild the setup.</p></details><details class="learning-question"><summary>What should a useful note after a round contain?</summary><p>The attack you tried, the response you saw, and which control or opening was present for the next action.</p></details>
</section>
