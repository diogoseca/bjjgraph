<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Different partners reveal which parts of your technique depend on a familiar reaction, reach, or pace.</p>
<p>The sweep that always works on your regular partner may meet a different post tomorrow. Keep the question you are practicing consistent enough to learn from that difference.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Choose a technical question before changing partners: can you keep inside position, finish an entry, or recover your guard?</li><li>Notice stance, grips, balance, and defensive choices before explaining the result through body size or belt rank.</li><li>Use familiar partners to refine an adjustment and different partners to test whether you can recognize when it applies.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>An unfamiliar partner still needs a clear agreement about pace and permitted techniques.</li><li>You can decline a pairing or ask for a different task; variety is useful only when both people can practice together.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Your closed guard sweep works when a familiar partner leans forward, but a new partner sits back.</h3><p>Identify the missing weight shift. Work on drawing a post or choose an attack suited to their posture rather than repeating the same sweep harder.</p><p class="learning-result">You learn the condition your sweep needs.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>A taller partner keeps reaching around your knee shield.</h3><p>Ask to restart from the same half guard and inspect the space between your knee, elbow, and their shoulder. Test an adjustment to the frame before changing your whole guard game.</p><p class="learning-result">The different reach gives you a concrete control problem to solve.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>A more experienced partner shuts down your pass before it begins.</h3><p>Ask them which grip or angle first stopped you. Restart there with a narrower task, such as establishing your passing grips while they deny them.</p><p class="learning-result">You get repetitions of the part you were missing instead of another round spent downstream of it.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Treating partners as difficulty levels.</h3><p>You miss the particular reactions that make each exchange useful.</p><p>Name what the partner does differently and practice responding to it.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Changing partner, technique, and starting position after every failure.</h3><p>You cannot tell which change mattered.</p><p>Keep the start and goal stable for several attempts, then compare.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more mistake</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>Assuming a technique is universal because it works on one person.</h3><p>A familiar opening can become an unnoticed requirement.</p><p>Test the same entry against another response and record where it changes.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Same start, different response</h3><p>Choose a closed guard sweep you already know. With each willing partner, start from the same grips and let them defend without passing. Reset after a sweep or a clearly stopped entry. Record their first defensive action and one adjustment to try.</p><p class="learning-focus">Compare responses rather than tally partners.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>What is worth recording after a round with someone new?</summary><p>The first grip, posture, or defensive response that changed your usual sequence, and the adjustment you want to test next.</p></details>
</section>
