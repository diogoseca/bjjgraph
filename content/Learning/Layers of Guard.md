<p class="learning-category">Strategy</p>
<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Think of guard layers as the barriers still separating a passer from your torso: feet and shins, knees, and arm frames.</p>
<p>When a foot gets cleared, look for what still works before trying to rebuild everything. These barriers overlap and change with the guard; there is no universal order that every pass must follow.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>At longer range, feet and shins can manage distance while your hips stay facing the passer.</li><li>As distance closes, a knee or shin between the bodies may preserve room to move your hips.</li><li>Arm frames can buy space for leg recovery, but reaching with both arms can leave your torso exposed.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>Keep one useful barrier while replacing another. Reaching for a distant foot placement may sacrifice the frame you already have.</li><li>An underhook is a positional choice, not a final layer that makes every guard safe.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>A standing passer knocks one foot off their hip</h3><p>Use the other leg and a suitable hand connection to manage distance as you turn to face them. Replace the cleared foot when there is room.</p><p class="learning-result">You repair one missing barrier without giving up the remaining one.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Your half guard knee shield is being folded down</h3><p>Keep your arms inside and prevent the passer from freely controlling your head. Make space to rebuild the shield or another guard connection.</p><p class="learning-result">You have something to work behind even if the original shield cannot be restored.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>The passer reaches your hip while your legs trail behind</h3><p>Use a frame to keep their torso from settling onto yours, then move your hips to bring a knee back inside. If they settle the pin, switch to an escape rather than reaching for their far hip with your foot.</p><p class="learning-result">You distinguish an available guard recovery from an established pin that needs different work.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Trying to put the feet back first at every distance</h3><p>The leg may have no path inside, and the reach opens the space beside your ribs.</p><p>Use the nearest workable frame or knee to make room before extending a leg.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Counting barriers instead of checking their function</h3><p>A foot touching the passer does little if it cannot affect distance or follow their movement.</p><p>Ask what the contact stops and whether your hips can still face the passer.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Replace one barrier</h3><p>Start in open guard with feet or shins making contact. Top clears one contact at a time while bottom follows and replaces it. Begin slowly, then add resistance; reset when top reaches a settled torso pin or bottom re-establishes both leg contacts.</p><p class="learning-focus">Keep a useful connection during the repair.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more exercise</summary><div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Close-range recovery</h3><p>Start with the passer beside the knees and bottom holding an arm frame, without a settled pin. Bottom tries to bring a knee inside; top tries to close the space. Reset on either result and switch sides.</p><p class="learning-focus">Use the remaining barrier to recover a stronger one.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>Must a passer clear feet, knees, arms, and an underhook in that order?</summary><p>No. Those contacts overlap, and passes can bypass some of them. Look at what blocks the passer in the actual position.</p></details><details class="learning-question"><summary>How does this differ from the phases of guard?</summary><p>Layers describe the physical barriers you have. Phases describe whether your immediate priority is engagement, attack, or recovery.</p></details>
</section>
