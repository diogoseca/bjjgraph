<p class="learning-category">Strategy</p>
<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">A good choice weighs the opening you can see, the likely response, and what happens if the attempt fails.</p>
<p>You do not need a percentage for every move. Before committing, ask whether the setup is present and whether you can handle the position you expect to reach if your partner defends.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Judge a technique in context: starting grips, partner behavior, rules, and your own familiarity all matter.</li><li>Consider the fallback as well as the finish. Two attacks from the same position can have very different costs when stopped.</li><li>Use training notes to find patterns, not to turn a handful of attempts into a universal success rate.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>A good decision can fail and a poor decision can work. Review the information available when you chose.</li><li>When score and time matter, choose an action that can meet the match objective; urgency does not create a missing setup.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>You can attack an armbar from mount or keep working a choke without leaving the top position.</h3><p>Compare your actual controls. If the armbar entry is loose and the opponent can pull their elbow free, improve the isolation first; a different grip configuration may make the armbar the clearer choice.</p><p class="learning-result">The decision follows the position rather than a blanket ranking of submissions.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>A sweep worked twice, so you decide it is your most reliable move.</h3><p>Record the start and the defenses you met. Test it against a different response before treating those successes as evidence that the setup is broadly available.</p><p class="learning-result">Your confidence remains tied to what you have actually tested.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>You are behind late in a points match.</h3><p>Identify what could change the result under that event’s rules, then choose an attack you can actually enter in the time available. Account for the opponent’s likely defensive priority.</p><p class="learning-result">You accept a purposeful risk instead of selecting a move merely because it looks dramatic.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Reading a displayed success estimate as your personal odds.</h3><p>The number may describe a different population or a simplified model, without your grips and opponent’s reaction.</p><p>Use it as context and assess the actual setup in front of you.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Changing your whole game after one failed attempt.</h3><p>A single result does not separate decision quality from execution or an unusual response.</p><p>Review the entry and the failure point, then retest the same question.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more mistake</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>Choosing the least risky option regardless of the goal.</h3><p>Keeping the current position may still lose a timed match or avoid the skill you meant to practice.</p><p>State the objective before comparing options.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Predict the fallback</h3><p>Start in a familiar attacking position. Before each attempt, name the likely defense and where you expect to land. Let your partner defend, then stop at the first settled position or tap. Compare the result with your prediction and reset.</p><p class="learning-focus">Notice costs that disappear when you count only finishes.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>What belongs beside an attempt count in your notes?</summary><p>The starting control, the response you met, and the position after failure; without that context, the count says little about your next decision.</p></details>
</section>
