<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Understanding a technique means recognizing when to use it, what control it needs, and what to change when the position changes.</p>
<p>Knowing the steps of an escape is useful, but a round does not announce which escape to use. Practice noticing one relevant detail before trying to recall an entire technique catalog.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Name the cue that makes a move available: a posted hand, a cleared knee, or a gap you can occupy.</li><li>Separate recognizing the position, choosing an action, and executing it. A failure in one does not identify a failure in all three.</li><li>Use a short cue you can carry into a round, then explain the full reasoning afterward.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>Test remembered details against a resisting partner. A correct written answer does not establish live skill.</li><li>Revise your cue when the same response appears in a different situation.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>You can describe a mount escape but cannot choose one during sparring.</h3><p>Start in mount bottom and notice whether the top partner has a hand available to post. Rehearse your taught trap-and-roll entry only when you can address that post; work another escape when you cannot.</p><p class="learning-result">The choice becomes tied to an observable obstacle.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>You know several passes and keep switching between them without progress.</h3><p>Pick one start and one question: are their knees still between your chest and theirs? Use the answer to distinguish an entry problem from a problem settling the pass.</p><p class="learning-result">You can work on the unfinished stage instead of starting another unrelated move.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>A flashcard answer feels obvious, but the position still confuses you.</h3><p>Recreate the pictured position with a partner. Have them change one detail, such as posture or a grip, and explain whether your planned action still fits.</p><p class="learning-result">You discover which details the written answer assumed.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Narrating every step internally while the round moves on.</h3><p>The explanation competes with the exchange you need to observe.</p><p>Carry one brief cue into the round and save detailed analysis for the reset.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Calling every failed attempt a bad decision.</h3><p>A sensible choice may have failed because of timing or execution.</p><p>Identify whether you misread the cue, chose an unsuitable action, or lost a required control.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Cue, attempt, review</h3><p>Start in a familiar position with a partner who can offer two agreed responses. Name the cue you see, attempt the matching action, and reset after the exchange. Once the cue is recognizable, repeat without speaking and review your read afterward.</p><p class="learning-focus">Connect a visible or felt change to a decision.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more exercise</summary><div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Rebuild it from memory</h3><p>After class, describe one taught technique without replaying the demonstration. In your next practice, reconstruct its starting grips and first action with a partner. Stop at the first uncertainty, check it with your coach, then restart from the corrected setup.</p><p class="learning-focus">Find the exact gap between recall and practice.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>How do you tell a reading problem from an execution problem?</summary><p>Reconstruct the start: was the opening present and did the chosen action fit it? If so, inspect where the grip, angle, or timing was lost during the attempt.</p></details>
</section>
