<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">An attack is harder to read when its early grips and movements also support another credible option.</p>
<p>You do not need theatrical feints. Start by noticing the extra signal you give before a favorite move: a unique grip, a long pause, or a shift that serves only that attack.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Use a setup that can lead to more than one action without dismantling your control.</li><li>Make the first threat useful on its own; if the opponent does not defend it, be prepared to continue.</li><li>Read a real change in their position before switching targets.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more takeaway</summary><ul><li>Conceal the choice of attack, not your partner’s chance to tap. Apply submissions with control.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Your partner retreats their weight every time you prepare a butterfly sweep</h3><p>Begin your sweep setup from the same posture and grips you use to pull them forward or follow them as they retreat. Choose the action once their weight changes.</p><p class="learning-result">The shared setup gives less advance notice of which direction you will use.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>A mounted opponent keeps both elbows close</h3><p>Develop a controlled collar-choke threat in the gi. If defending it moves an elbow away from the ribs, consider an arm attack only when your base and control support it; otherwise continue the choke setup or rebuild position.</p><p class="learning-result">A second target can emerge from a genuine defense, without assuming the arm must become available.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>Your preferred passing side is obvious before you move</h3><p>Use a balanced stance and useful leg controls that allow an approach to either side. Change direction when their frames commit, keeping track of the returning knee.</p><p class="learning-result">Your first grip no longer announces the entire pass.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Faking an attack that cannot affect the opponent</h3><p>They have no reason to change their position.</p><p>Improve the first attack until it deserves a response.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Switching because the combination says to, despite no opening</h3><p>You leave a workable attack for a second one with missing controls.</p><p>Continue the first threat or recover your position when the expected defense does not appear.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Same start, different choice</h3><p>Pick two familiar actions from the same guard with overlapping grips. Show your partner the shared starting position, then let them defend freely. Choose from the actual response; reset on a sweep, pass, lost guard, or tap.</p><p class="learning-focus">Reduce unnecessary signals without losing a functional setup.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more exercise</summary><div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Find the tell</h3><p>Record a short positional round with your partner’s agreement. Find the earliest moment they could identify your favorite attack, then repeat from that position with one unnecessary movement removed. Stop and reset after the attack or defense is resolved.</p><p class="learning-focus">Separate essential preparation from avoidable telegraphing.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>What should you do when the first attack is not defended?</summary><p>Continue it if the controls are sound. The second attack is an option, not an obligation.</p></details>
</section>
