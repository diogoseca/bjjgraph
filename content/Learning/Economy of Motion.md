<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Economy of motion means using movement to change the position, rather than repeatedly spending effort on an obstacle you have not removed.</p>
<p>If you have bridged three times and landed in the same pin, a harder fourth attempt may not answer the problem. Look at what stayed fixed and what space the next movement needs to create.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Give each grip a job. Release or replace it when it no longer controls something useful.</li><li>Connect movements: use the space a bridge creates for the next action instead of returning to the same shape.</li><li>Adjust your support as weight moves rather than holding one rigid posture through the whole exchange.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>Efficient does not always mean small or slow. A large movement can be appropriate when it solves the actual problem.</li><li>Use the result to judge effort: did you improve your angle, recover a barrier, or remove a post?</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>You keep pushing a side-control partner away with your arms.</h3><p>Inspect where your elbows and hips are. Rebuild the frames your coach has taught, then use the space they preserve to move your hips and recover a knee.</p><p class="learning-result">The arm effort supports a specific recovery instead of an indefinite push.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>You grip a sleeve tightly after your partner has already changed position.</h3><p>Ask what the grip now prevents. If it no longer limits their movement or helps your attack, replace it with a grip that serves the current exchange.</p><p class="learning-result">Your hands stop working to preserve a connection you no longer need.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>You circle around open guard but the opponent turns with every step.</h3><p>First control a leg or create an angle that makes their turn harder. Move when that control changes the relationship between their knees and your path.</p><p class="learning-result">You can judge the pass by progress rather than the distance you ran.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Trying to win a prize for moving the least.</h3><p>Stillness can let the opponent settle a grip or pin.</p><p>Move as much as the position requires; remove repetition that does not change it.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Relaxing every muscle because frames should carry the load.</h3><p>A frame can collapse or drift out of place when you stop managing its alignment.</p><p>Use the tension needed to keep the frame useful, and adjust it as the force changes.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Find the wasted repetition</h3><p>Start in a familiar pin with agreed resistance. Attempt your taught escape and reset after recovery or a stopped attempt. Have your partner identify a repeated movement that left the position unchanged; alter its setup or follow-up on the next attempt.</p><p class="learning-focus">Link effort to a visible change in position.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>When is a large movement economical?</summary><p>When its size is needed to create the space, angle, or off-balance you can actually use, without unnecessary repetitions.</p></details>
</section>
