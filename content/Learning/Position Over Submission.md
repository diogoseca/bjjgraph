<p class="learning-category">Strategy</p>
<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">Before and during a submission attempt, maintain the controls that stop your opponent from escaping the attack.</p>
<p>Being in mount is not enough if reaching for a choke removes your only post. The useful question is whether you can make the attacking movement while handling the escape your partner is already attempting.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Name the opponent’s available escape before moving a hand or leg into the attack.</li><li>Control is specific to the submission: inspect posture, limb isolation, and the direction the opponent can move.</li><li>Guard attacks need control too. Being underneath does not make an attack poor, and being on top does not make it secure.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>Use the opponent’s defense to adjust your position; do not require them to stop moving before you begin attacking.</li><li>Take a clear opening when its controls are present. There is no compulsory waiting period before every submission.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>You arrive in side control while your partner is already bringing a knee inside.</h3><p>Address the knee and hip movement before committing both hands to an arm attack. If the guard is recovering, respond to that exchange rather than acting as though the pass is complete.</p><p class="learning-result">Your next attack starts from the control you actually have.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>You reach for a collar choke from mount and get rolled.</h3><p>Recreate the reach with a partner who bridges. Check which hand or leg supplies balance and adjust your base or grip sequence before repeating the choke entry.</p><p class="learning-result">You practice making the attack without ignoring the active escape.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>You see an armbar from closed guard, but the opponent can withdraw their arm as soon as you turn.</h3><p>Work on the arm control and angle your version of the entry requires. If that control disappears, recover your guard or follow the new opening instead of throwing your leg across an empty target.</p><p class="learning-result">The attack is connected to an isolated limb rather than the name of a position.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Waiting passively for the opponent to tire before attacking.</h3><p>They can use that time to rebuild frames and organize an escape.</p><p>Continue improving control and use reactions that create an available attack.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Treating mount or back control as proof that any submission entry is sound.</h3><p>Moving into the finish can surrender the very control you relied on.</p><p>Rehearse the entry against the escape most likely to interrupt it.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more mistake</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>Refusing a clear submission because another position ranks higher.</h3><p>You can pass up an opening that already has the necessary control.</p><p>Judge the actual isolation and escape routes, not only the positional label.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Attack while they escape</h3><p>Start in mount with a familiar submission entry. The bottom partner uses one agreed escape as you enter. Stop before finishing pressure, after a positional escape, or on any tap; reset and change the grip or base that failed. Add a second escape option when the first exchange is understood.</p><p class="learning-focus">Maintain relevant control during the entry.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>What should you check before committing both hands to a choke?</summary><p>Whether the grip is useful and how you will maintain balance and connection against the opponent’s available escape.</p></details>
