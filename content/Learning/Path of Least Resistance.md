<section id="overview" class="learning-section">
<p class="learning-summary">When a defense blocks one route, look for a better angle or target while keeping the control that makes a new attack possible.</p>
<p>A stiff frame is information about the direction it resists. Sometimes you can move around it; sometimes you need to remove it, or stop attacking and restore your balance first.</p>
</section>


<section id="key-takeaways" class="learning-section">
<h2>Key takeaways</h2>
<ul><li>Identify the obstacle before changing technique: a post, frame, hook, grip, or body angle.</li><li>Change one useful thing at a time, such as your angle around a frame or the direction of an off-balance.</li><li>Keep the grips and connections that still serve the next action.</li></ul>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 2 more takeaways</summary><ul><li>Do not confuse an easier-looking opening with a safer one; check what a switch exposes.</li><li>Redirect after a meaningful attempt, not at the first sign that the opponent will resist.</li></ul></details>

</section>



<section id="applications" class="learning-section">
<h2>In practice</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>An open guard player’s feet stop your forward passing pressure</h3><p>Keep your balance and work to redirect or clear a leg before approaching from the side. Driving harder into both extended legs repeats the same problem.</p><p class="learning-result">The next attempt addresses the barrier instead of adding force in its strongest direction.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Your butterfly sweep meets a firm post</h3><p>Notice which arm or leg is supporting them. Keep your upper-body connection while changing the angle or preparing an attack that deals with that post.</p><p class="learning-result">The defense tells you what must change before another sweep attempt.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more example</summary><div class="learning-example"><h3>A mount attack draws both hands to the neck</h3><p>Look for an exposed elbow while preserving your base. If the elbows remain tight, improve the existing choke grips or position instead of reaching for an arm that is still protected.</p><p class="learning-result">You use an available opening without treating a neck defense as an automatic armbar.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="mistakes" class="learning-section">
<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<div class="learning-example"><h3>Changing attacks before establishing any control</h3><p>Every new move starts from a worse connection than the last.</p><p>Keep the current setup until you can name the barrier and a workable replacement.</p></div><div class="learning-example"><h3>Backstepping over a trapped leg just to avoid a knee shield</h3><p>Your leg and balance may remain controlled as your torso turns away.</p><p>Free or account for the entanglement before changing your passing direction.</p></div>

</section>



<section id="exercises" class="learning-section">
<h2>Try it in training</h2>
<div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Find the barrier</h3><p>Start with a familiar guard pass and let bottom provide one clear defense. Pause before forcing through it, identify the obstructing contact, then try an angle change. Reset when the pass settles or guard is recovered; increase resistance once the route is clear.</p><p class="learning-focus">Choose a change that addresses the actual obstruction.</p></div>

<details class="learning-more"><summary>Show 1 more exercise</summary><div class="learning-exercise"><h3>Keep one connection</h3><p>Start in butterfly guard with an agreed upper-body grip. Top defends the first sweep with a post; bottom explores a second direction while retaining useful contact. Reset on a sweep, pass, lost connection, or tap.</p><p class="learning-focus">Redirect without starting the whole exchange again.</p></div></details>

</section>



<section id="self-assessment" class="learning-section">
<h2>Self-assessment</h2>
<details class="learning-question"><summary>Should resistance always make you switch techniques?</summary><p>No. First check whether the original attack lacks a grip, angle, or balance break that you can still obtain.</p></details>
</section>
