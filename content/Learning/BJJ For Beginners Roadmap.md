# Beginner's Roadmap: First 12 Months

**Your first year of Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ) breaks into four phases: months 0-3 you learn five core positions ([[Positions/Mount]], [[Positions/Side Control]], [[Positions/Closed Guard]], [[Positions/Back Control]], and [[Positions/Turtle]]); months 3-6 you add escapes, two sweeps, and two submissions; months 6-9 you connect techniques into chains; and months 9-12 you refine a game plan for blue belt.** Start at 2-3 sessions per week and build toward 4-5. Consistency matters more than athleticism, and most students reach blue belt in one to two years.

Your first day will be humbling. Everyone who walks into a BJJ academy -- regardless of athletic background -- gets dominated. This is normal. Here's a structured path through your first year.

## Your First Classes

**Typical class structure:**
- Warm-up (10-15 min): shrimping, bridging, forward rolls, and the [[Transitions/Technical Stand Up from Ground|technical standup]]
- Technique instruction (20-30 min): instructor demonstrates, you drill with a partner
- Positional sparring (15-20 min): practice under controlled resistance
- Live rolling (10-20 min): full resistance rounds (beginners often sit these out initially)

**Survival tips:**
- Breathe through your nose. Panic breathing exhausts you in 30 seconds.
- Tap early and often. Ego injuries heal; joint injuries don't.
- Don't muscle through techniques. Relax and feel the movement.
- Pick one detail per class to remember. You won't retain everything.

Your goal for month one is showing up consistently. Nothing else matters yet.

## Months 0-3: Five Positions

Before learning any techniques, you need to know where you are. Focus exclusively on these five positions:

**1. [[Positions/Mount]] -- Top and Bottom** (40% of your positional work)
The most dominant position. Top: learn to maintain without hands and apply pressure through a solid [[Principles/Base]]. Bottom: learn the [[Transitions/Trap and Roll from Mount|bridge-and-roll (upa) escape]] and the [[Transitions/Elbow Escape from Mount|elbow-knee escape]] to recover half guard.

**2. [[Positions/Side Control]] -- Top and Bottom** (30%)
The most common transition point. Every guard pass ends here. Top: distribute weight and control the near hip. Bottom: build [[Principles/Frames]] on the neck and hip, then [[Transitions/Hip Escape from Side Control|hip escape]] to recover guard.

**3. [[Positions/Closed Guard]] -- Top and Bottom** (20%)
Your primary defensive position. Bottom: break [[Principles/Posture|posture]], maintain control. Top: establish strong posture, open the guard, begin passing.

**4. [[Positions/Back Control]]** (5%)
Highest-percentage submission position. Learn to maintain with hooks and seatbelt grip, learn the [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]], and learn to defend your neck when someone has your back.

**5. [[Positions/Turtle]]** (5%)
Where you end up when your guard gets passed. Learn to protect your neck, prevent the back take, and return to guard.

At this stage, just train 2-3 times per week. Focus on recognizing positions during rolling. After each round, ask yourself: "which position was I in?"

## Months 3-6: Core Techniques

Once you can identify positions, learn the fundamental techniques that connect them.

### Escapes (learn these first)

**Mount escape**: Trap the arm and leg on the same side, then bridge and roll -- the [[Transitions/Trap and Roll from Mount]] (also called the **upa**). When the opponent stays low and based out, switch to the [[Transitions/Elbow Escape from Mount|elbow-knee escape]]. Practice both until they're automatic -- you'll use them hundreds of times.

**Side control escape**: Create [[Principles/Frames|frames]], [[Transitions/Hip Escape from Side Control|hip escape]] to create space, insert a knee shield, and recover to [[Positions/Half Guard]] or full guard. Focus on shrimp quality over speed.

**Back escape**: Hand fight to prevent the choke, clear one hook by lifting your hip, then turn into your opponent to face them.

### Sweeps from Guard

**[[Transitions/Hip Bump Sweep]]**: Sit up from closed guard, control the opponent's arm, and bump them backward with your hips. Mechanically simple, works well for beginners.

**[[Transitions/Scissor Sweep]]**: One leg across the waist, the other behind the knee, pull forward and scissor. Teaches proper use of the legs for off-balancing.

### Submissions (pick two, ignore the rest)

**Option A: [[Submissions/Armbar]] from closed guard** -- works when you control distance and get a good hip angle.

**Option B: [[Submissions/Kimura]]** -- works from guard, half guard, and scrambles. Very forgiving, with multiple finishing positions.

**Option C: [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]]** -- the highest-percentage submission in BJJ. If you can take the back, this is your finish.

### Guard Passing

**[[Transitions/Knee Slice Pass]]**: Slice your knee across the opponent's thigh while controlling their legs. Teaches pressure and direction.

Increase training to 3-4 times per week. Start drilling your two chosen submissions repetitively.

## Months 6-9: Building Chains

BJJ isn't about isolated techniques -- it's about connecting them into sequences where defending one attack opens another.

### Your First Attack Chain

Build a sequence from one position. Example from [[Positions/Closed Guard]]:
1. Break posture with collar and sleeve grips
2. Attempt the [[Submissions/Armbar]]
3. If they stack to defend, [[Transitions/Switch to Triangle|switch to the triangle]] ([[Submissions/Triangle Choke]])
4. If they posture out of the triangle, [[Transitions/Triangle to Omoplata|transition to the omoplata]] ([[Submissions/Omoplata]])
5. If they roll out, take [[Positions/Back Control]]

Pick one position and build a chain like this. Drill the *connections* between techniques, not just individual moves.

### Your First Defensive Chain

Example from bottom [[Positions/Side Control]]:
1. Establish [[Principles/Frames|frames]] (forearms on neck and hip)
2. [[Transitions/Hip Escape from Side Control|Hip escape]] to create space
3. Insert a knee shield
4. Recover to [[Positions/Half Guard]]
5. Work an underhook or lockdown from half guard

### Position-Specific Goals

- **Guard (bottom)**: attempt a sweep or submission within 2 minutes. Never be passive.
- **Passing (top)**: pass to side control within 3 minutes.
- **Top control**: hold position for 1 minute before attacking submissions.
- **Bottom (bad position)**: escape within 30 seconds or frame and survive until an opportunity opens.

Add open mat sessions for extra rolling. Start identifying your style -- are you a guard player or a passer?

## Months 9-12: Blue Belt Preparation

The final quarter is about consistency and refinement, not learning new techniques.

### What Blue Belt Actually Means

Blue belt is not "advanced beginner." It means you can:
- Submit untrained people reliably
- Survive against higher belts without being immediately submitted
- Execute a coherent game plan from major positions
- Help newer students understand fundamentals
- Roll for 30+ minutes without breaking down

### Finding Your Style

By now, your natural game is emerging:

**Guard player**: comfortable on bottom, good leg dexterity, attacks from guard. Develop [[Positions/Open Guard]] variations and sweep chains.

**Pressure passer**: prefers top, good [[Principles/Base|base]], patient and methodical. Perfect smash passing and submission chains from [[Positions/Mount]] and [[Positions/Side Control]].

**Scrambler**: thrives in chaos, quick reflexes. Develop the [[Transitions/Back Take]] from transitions and leg entanglements.

Don't force a style. Observe where you feel comfortable and develop from there.

### Signs You're Ready

- You consistently sweep or submit other white belts
- Upper belts no longer tap you in the first 30 seconds
- You have go-to moves from every major position
- You recognize submission threats before they're fully applied
- You've stopped using 100% strength
- You can explain *why* techniques work, not just how

## Common Questions

**How long does it take to get a blue belt in BJJ?**
Most students earn their blue belt after one to two years of consistent training, typically 2-4 sessions per week. There is no fixed timeline -- promotion depends on your instructor's standards, your attendance, and your ability to apply fundamentals against resisting partners.

**How often should a BJJ beginner train?**
Start with 2-3 classes per week for the first few months to let your body adapt and avoid burnout. As your conditioning and recovery improve (usually around months 3-6), build up to 3-4 sessions, adding open-mat rolling once you have a base of techniques.

**What should you learn first in BJJ?**
Learn positions before techniques. Spend your first three months recognizing and holding the five core positions -- [[Positions/Mount]], [[Positions/Side Control]], [[Positions/Closed Guard]], [[Positions/Back Control]], and [[Positions/Turtle]] -- then add escapes, because escaping bad positions keeps you safe and in the match longer than any attack.

**Which submissions should a white belt focus on?**
Pick two and drill them deeply rather than collecting many. The [[Submissions/Armbar]], [[Submissions/Kimura]], and [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]] are the highest-value beginner submissions because they appear from many positions and are forgiving of small mistakes.

**Is BJJ hard for beginners?**
Yes -- your first months will feel humbling because every beginner gets dominated regardless of athletic background. That difficulty is normal and temporary. Progress comes from consistent attendance, tapping early to avoid injury, and focusing on survival and position before chasing submissions.

**What is the difference between a guard player and a passer?**
A guard player is comfortable attacking from the bottom with sweeps and submissions from positions like [[Positions/Closed Guard]] and [[Positions/Open Guard]], while a passer prefers to be on top, using [[Principles/Base|base]] and pressure to get past the legs into [[Positions/Side Control]] and [[Positions/Mount]]. Most beginners develop a preference by months 6-9.
