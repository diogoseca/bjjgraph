# How to Pass the Guard in BJJ?

**Guard passing** is the primary top-game objective in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu — the systematic process of advancing from bottom-of-guard to a dominant position like [[Side Control]], [[Mount]], or [[Back Control]]. Effective guard passing requires chaining multiple techniques based on opponent reactions rather than relying on a single technique. Success rates range from 40–55% for beginners to 70–85% for advanced practitioners using systematic passing chains.

## What Makes Guard Passing Different from Other BJJ Skills?

Guard passing differs fundamentally from other BJJ techniques. While a sweep or submission represents a single decisive action, passing the guard is a continuous process of problem-solving against an opponent actively working to maintain guard. The passer must navigate distance management, grip fighting, base disruption attempts, and re-guarding sequences while systematically advancing toward dominant position.

Successful passing requires understanding that no single technique works in isolation. Instead, effective passers develop chains of connected passes, using each defensive reaction to set up the next passing opportunity. This systematic approach transforms guard passing from a series of disconnected techniques into a cohesive offensive strategy.

## What Are the Core Principles of Guard Passing?

### Why Is Posture Critical for Guard Passing?

Maintaining proper posture is the foundation of all guard passing. Against [[Closed Guard]], strong posture prevents effective attacks and creates passing opportunities. The passer's spine should remain aligned, head up, shoulders back, creating a frame that's difficult to break down. Loss of posture immediately shifts advantage to the guard player, enabling sweeps and submissions.

Posture requirements vary by guard type. In [[Open Guard]] scenarios, dynamic posture allows quick direction changes while maintaining defensive frames. Against [[Top Half Guard]], the passer often uses forward pressure combined with head position to prevent underhooks and establish control.

### How Does Base and Balance Affect Guard Passing?

Stable base prevents sweeps and creates a platform for pressure application. The passer's feet, knees, and hands form triangulation points that distribute weight effectively and resist off-balancing attempts. Wide base provides stability but limits mobility; narrow base enables quick movement but increases sweep vulnerability.

Advanced passers constantly adjust base width based on immediate threats. When defending [[Butterfly Guard]] hooks, wider base neutralizes elevation sweeps. During [[Knee Slice Pass]] sequences, the passer narrows base momentarily to slice through, then immediately re-establishes wide base in the guard player's hip line.

### Should You Use Pressure or Speed When Passing the Guard?

The pressure vs. speed dichotomy defines passing philosophy. **Pressure passing** emphasizes control, grinding past guards through superior positioning and weight distribution. **Speed passing** relies on quick direction changes, exploiting brief defensive openings before the guard player can adjust.

Pressure passing generally yields higher success rates (55–70% vs. 50–65% for speed passing) because it maintains dominant position throughout the passing sequence. However, speed passing proves more effective against certain guards like [[De La Riva Guard]] where hooks create leverage that reduces pressure effectiveness.

Elite passers blend both approaches, using pressure when advantageous and speed when pressure proves ineffective. [[Leg Drag Control]] exemplifies this hybrid approach — initial entry uses speed to clear legs, followed by pressure-based consolidation in side control.

### How Do You Manage Distance When Passing the Guard?

Controlling distance prevents guard players from establishing optimal frames and hook positions. Too close allows strong frames and submission threats; too far enables guard retention through mobility and re-guarding. Effective passers manipulate distance strategically, closing when controlling grips and hips, extending when the guard player achieves advantageous frames.

[[Knee Shield Half Guard]] demonstrates distance management importance. The passer must collapse the knee shield frame to enter pressure passing range while preventing the guard player from creating space for sweeps or guard recovery.

## How Do You Pass Different Types of Guards?

### How Do You Pass the Closed Guard?

[[Closed Guard]] presents unique challenges as the guard player's legs create a complete circuit of control. Opening the guard requires systematic breaking of ankle lock and posture control, typically through standing techniques, knee positioning, or grip manipulation.

The standing guard break achieves highest success rates (60–75% for advanced practitioners) by leveraging leg strength against hip flexibility. Once open, the passer immediately transitions to open guard passing sequences before the guard player can re-close or establish open guard controls.

Common passing sequences from closed guard:
- Stand and break → [[Toreando Pass]] → [[Side Control]] (Success Rate: Beginner 45%, Intermediate 60%, Advanced 75%)
- Knee position break → [[Knee Slice Pass]] → [[Side Control]] (Success Rate: Beginner 50%, Intermediate 65%, Advanced 75%)
- Pressure break → [[Stack Pass]] → [[Side Control]] (Success Rate: Beginner 40%, Intermediate 55%, Advanced 70%)

### How Do You Pass Open Guard?

Open guard encompasses numerous variations, each requiring specific passing approaches. The fundamental principle remains constant: control the guard player's hips and legs while advancing toward side control or mount.

[[Spider Guard]] passing emphasizes grip breaking and posture control. The passer must systematically remove foot-on-bicep controls while maintaining distance that prevents triangle and omoplata entries. Success rates for spider guard passing range from 45–65% depending on skill level and specific passing technique.

[[De La Riva Guard]] requires strategies focused on preventing back exposure and controlling the hooking leg. [[Leg Drag Control]] proves particularly effective, using the DLR hook itself as a handle to clear the guard. Advanced DLR passing achieves 55–70% success rates through systematic leg control and hip positioning.

### How Do You Pass Half Guard?

[[Top Half Guard]] represents one of the most common passing scenarios in modern BJJ. The trapped leg creates immediate pressure on the bottom player while providing the passer multiple attacking directions.

Effective half guard passing addresses three primary obstacles:
1. Underhook prevention and removal
2. Knee shield frame collapse
3. Lockdown control (if applicable)

[[Knee Slice from Half]] demonstrates systematic half guard passing: establish cross-face control, collapse knee shield, slice trapped leg free while driving chest pressure, consolidate in side control. This sequence achieves 60–75% success rates at advanced levels. [[Underhook Pass]] offers an alternative when the passer obtains the underhook first (55–70% success rate).

### How Do You Pass Leg Entanglement Guards?

Modern leg lock meta has elevated leg entanglement guards like [[50-50 Guard]], [[Ashi Garami]], and [[Single Leg X-Guard]]. Passing these guards requires understanding leg lock mechanics and systematic leg extraction.

The key principle: prevent the opponent's heel hook control while systematically freeing your trapped leg. This often involves accepting transitional exposure to less dangerous positions (straight footlock range) to escape high-danger positions (heel hook range).

Success rates for leg entanglement passing vary dramatically (30–45% beginner, 50–65% advanced) due to the technical complexity and submission danger involved.

## What Are the Highest-Percentage Guard Passes?

### Knee Slice Pass

The [[Knee Slice Pass]] is the highest-percentage pass across all skill levels (Beginner 55%, Intermediate 65%, Advanced 75%). Its effectiveness derives from multiple factors:
- Applicable against most guard types
- Utilizes full body weight for pressure
- Creates immediate side control threat
- Chains naturally to other passes when defended

Proper knee slice execution requires controlling the guard player's near hip, driving the cutting knee across the thigh, and using chest pressure to flatten the opponent. The pass succeeds even when partially defended by creating favorable [[Knee on Belly]] or [[Top Half Guard]] positions.

### Toreando Pass

The [[Toreando Pass]] emphasizes speed and grip control, throwing the guard player's legs to one side while circling to the opposite direction. Success rates range from 50–70% depending on timing and grip dominance.

The toreando excels against [[Butterfly Guard]] and [[Open Guard]] where the guard player relies on framing and mobility rather than hook-based control. It chains effectively with [[Leg Drag Control]] when the opponent attempts to square up.

### Leg Drag

[[Leg Drag Control]] creates asymmetry by controlling both the guard player's legs to one side of their body, enabling the passer to move to the opposite side with minimal resistance. This pass achieves 55–70% success rates and proves particularly effective against [[De La Riva Guard]] and [[Reverse De La Riva Guard]].

The leg drag's power comes from its ability to bypass the guard player's frames entirely, attacking from an angle where defensive frames cannot reach. Advanced practitioners chain leg drags with [[Back Control to Crucifix]] when the opponent turns away to defend.

### Smash Pass

The [[Smash Pass]] uses overwhelming shoulder and chest pressure to flatten the guard player, removing their ability to create angles or maintain frames. This pass works exceptionally well in gi BJJ where lapel grips enhance control (60–75% success rate at advanced level).

Smash passing proves most effective against [[Top Half Guard]] and [[Butterfly Guard]] positions where the passer can establish dominant cross-face control early in the sequence.

### Stack Pass

The [[Stack Pass]] lifts the guard player's hips high, loading weight onto their shoulders and creating acute folding that compromises defensive capability. Success rates range from 50–70%, with effectiveness varying based on the passer's strength and the opponent's flexibility.

Stack passing works particularly well against [[Closed Guard]] after opening and [[Spider Guard]] where the guard player's extended legs create natural stacking angles. The pass chains naturally to [[Mount]] when the guard player attempts to prevent side control by turning.

### Long Step Pass

The long step or "leg weave" pass involves stepping one leg deep between the guard player's legs, using that positioning to control hips and prevent re-guarding. This technical pass achieves 50–65% success rates and proves highly effective against [[Butterfly Guard]] and [[X-Guard]].

Long step passing requires precise timing and balance, making it more challenging for beginners but exceptionally effective at advanced levels where practitioners can read and counter defensive reactions quickly.

## How Do You Build Effective Passing Chains?

Individual passes rarely succeed in isolation against skilled opponents. Effective passers develop chains where each passing attempt sets up the next based on defensive reactions.

**Example Passing Chain:**
1. Attempt [[Knee Slice Pass]]
2. If opponent frames and creates space → switch to [[Toreando Pass]]
3. If opponent attempts to square up → transition to [[Leg Drag Control]]
4. If opponent turns away → pursue [[Back Control to Crucifix]]

This chain covers multiple defensive options, ensuring the passer maintains forward pressure regardless of the guard player's response. Advanced practitioners develop 3–4 such chains that address all common defensive reactions.

### Passing Strategy by Opponent Type

**Against Flexible Opponents:**
- Emphasize pressure passing over speed
- Favor [[Smash Pass]] and [[Stack Pass]]
- Avoid positions where flexibility creates defensive advantages
- Success improvement: 15–20% through proper technique selection

**Against Strong Opponents:**
- Use technical passes that minimize strength requirements
- Favor [[Toreando Pass]] and [[Leg Drag Control]]
- Maintain distance to prevent them from establishing advantageous frames
- Success improvement: 10–15% through strategic selection

**Against Quick Opponents:**
- Emphasize grip control and pressure-based passing
- Slow the pace through dominant grips and positional control
- Favor [[Knee Slice Pass]] and [[Smash Pass]]
- Success improvement: 15–25% by neutralizing speed advantage

## What Are the Most Common Guard Passing Mistakes?

### Committing to One Pass Without a Backup Plan

The most prevalent error involves committing fully to one pass without preparing backup options. When the guard player successfully defends, the passer has no continuation, leading to stalling or forced guard recovery.

**Fix:** Develop passing chains where each technique naturally transitions to alternatives based on defensive reactions. Never attempt a pass without planning the next 2–3 options.

### Losing Base During Passing Transitions

Passers often sacrifice base stability during transitions, creating sweep opportunities. This occurs most frequently during [[Toreando Pass]] attempts where the passer over-commits to one direction.

**Fix:** Maintain triangulated base throughout passing sequences. When base must narrow (as in knee cut entries), immediately re-establish wide base in the opponent's hip line.

### Assuming the Pass Is Complete Too Early

Assuming the pass is complete before achieving full control leads to guard recovery or scrambles. This occurs frequently during [[Knee Slice Pass]] sequences where the passer's knee clears the legs but side control isn't yet secured.

**Fix:** Passes aren't complete until full side control with the opponent flat on their back. Maintain pressure and control through the entire transition to [[Side Control]].

### Neglecting Grip Fighting

Failing to win crucial grips before attempting passes significantly reduces success rates (15–25% reduction). Guard players with dominant grips can prevent passing entries entirely.

**Fix:** Prioritize grip fighting before committing to passing sequences. The first engagement should establish dominant grips that facilitate your passing system while denying the opponent's preferred guards.

### Energy Inefficiency

Attempting low-percentage passes or repeatedly failing the same technique drains energy. This creates negative feedback where fatigue further reduces passing effectiveness.

**Fix:** Select high-percentage passes appropriate to the guard type and opponent characteristics. Abandon techniques after 1–2 failed attempts, transitioning to alternatives rather than forcing ineffective approaches.

## Which Guard Passing System Should You Use?

### Systematic Approach

Building passing systems around a few core passes that chain together based on defensive reactions proves highly effective. This approach prioritizes understanding positional relationships and control points over accumulating numerous isolated techniques.

- Master 3–4 passes that cover all major guard types
- Develop clear if/then decision trees for defensive reactions
- Emphasize achieving dominant positions before seeking submissions
- Systematic passing achieves 70–85% success rates at advanced levels

### Pressure-Based Systems

Competition-proven pressure passing built around [[Top Half Guard]] and [[Smash Pass]] offers a reliable path to success. These systems emphasize over-under pass variations, cross-face control maintenance, and transitioning pressure between positions during defensive attempts.

Pressure-based approaches prove particularly effective for smaller practitioners who must overcome strength disadvantages through superior technique and pressure application principles.

### Speed-Based Systems

High-level speed passing focused on [[Toreando Pass]] and [[Leg Drag Control]] chains relies on explosive direction changes, grip manipulation that disrupts opponent's base, and immediately capitalizing on brief defensive openings.

Speed passing systems typically require higher physical conditioning but can achieve 65–75% success rates against opponents with reactive rather than proactive guards.

## How Do You Develop Your Guard Passing Game?

### Skill Level Progression

**Beginner (0–2 years):**
- Focus on [[Knee Slice Pass]] as primary technique
- Learn basic pressure passing principles
- Develop posture and base fundamentals
- Study passing against [[Closed Guard]] and basic [[Open Guard]]
- Target success rate: 40–55% against similar skill level

**Intermediate (2–4 years):**
- Add [[Toreando Pass]] and [[Leg Drag Control]] to create passing chains
- Study guard-specific passing strategies
- Develop pressure vs. speed selection based on opponent analysis
- Begin incorporating [[Top Half Guard]] pass specialization
- Target success rate: 55–70% against similar skill level

**Advanced (4+ years):**
- Master complete passing chains covering all defensive reactions
- Develop expert system in one passing style (pressure or speed)
- Add secondary system for style-matching purposes
- Study leg entanglement passing for modern meta
- Target success rate: 70–85% against similar skill level

### Training Methodology

**Positional Sparring:** Dedicate 40–50% of training time to specific passing situations. Start from established guard positions with 3–5 minute rounds focused exclusively on passing vs. guard retention. This develops the problem-solving skills essential for effective passing.

**Flow Drilling:** Practice passing chains in flow state without resistance. This builds muscle memory for transitioning between passes based on defensive reactions. 10–15 minutes per session maintains technique quality while developing automatic responses.

**Technique Study:** Regularly analyze high-level competition footage, studying how elite passers chain techniques and respond to defenses. This develops strategic understanding that accelerates practical skill development.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Guard Passing

### What is the highest-percentage guard pass in BJJ?

The [[Knee Slice Pass]] is the highest-percentage pass across all skill levels, with success rates of 55% for beginners, 65% for intermediate, and 75% for advanced practitioners. Its effectiveness comes from applicability against most guard types, full body weight utilization, and natural chaining to other passes when defended.

### What is the difference between pressure passing and speed passing in BJJ?

Pressure passing uses body weight and superior positioning to grind past guards (55–70% success rate), prioritizing control throughout the sequence. Speed passing uses explosive direction changes to exploit brief openings before the guard player can react (50–65% success rate). Elite passers blend both: using pressure when advantageous and speed when the guard creates leverage that neutralizes pressure.

### How long does it take to develop a good guard passing game?

Developing a functional passing game takes 2–4 years minimum. Beginners (0–2 years) should focus on one primary pass ([[Knee Slice Pass]]) with a 40–55% success rate goal. Intermediate players (2–4 years) should develop 2–3 pass chains at 55–70% success. Advanced practitioners (4+ years) target 70–85% success through systematic passing chains covering all defensive reactions.

### How do I pass the guard against someone with strong or flexible legs?

Against flexible or strong-legged opponents, favor pressure passing ([[Smash Pass]], [[Stack Pass]]) over speed passing. Stack passing directly exploits strong legs by using them as levers to fold the opponent. Maintain tight posture to prevent hooks from establishing, and use over-under grips to control hip movement before committing to the pass.

### What should I do when every guard pass I attempt gets defended?

When individual passes fail consistently, switch to systematic passing chains rather than repeating the same technique. After a failed [[Toreando Pass]], transition to [[Leg Drag Control]]; when the leg drag is defended, use the opponent's defensive movement to enter [[Back Control to Crucifix]]. Every defensive reaction creates an opportunity for the next technique in the chain.

### Why do I keep getting swept when trying to pass the guard?

Getting swept during passing attempts usually results from compromised base. The most common causes: (1) over-committing to one direction during [[Toreando Pass]], (2) losing base when the knee cuts during [[Knee Slice Pass]], or (3) failing to maintain triangulated base during transitions. Prioritize wide base throughout all passing sequences; even when narrowing base is temporarily necessary, immediately re-establish wide base in the opponent's hip line.

### Is pressure passing or speed passing better for beginners?

Pressure passing is better for beginners because it requires less precise timing and builds fundamental base and weight distribution skills. Focus on [[Knee Slice Pass]] first as it combines elements of both pressure and speed. Speed passing requires timing sensitivity that develops only with experience; beginners attempting speed passes often over-commit and get swept.

### How do I pass leg entanglement guards like 50-50 safely?

Passing leg entanglement guards ([[50-50 Guard]], [[Ashi Garami]], [[Single Leg X-Guard]]) requires prioritizing heel hook prevention over immediate passing. Keep your feet out of heel hook range by pointing toes away from the opponent. Accept straight ankle lock exposure to escape heel hook danger. Work systematically to extract your trapped leg while maintaining a wide base, and never extend your captured leg while it remains entangled.
