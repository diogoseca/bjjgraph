**Game Over** is the terminal state in the BJJ Graph — the single sink node that every submission technique leads to. When a competitor taps out, verbally submits, or is stopped by a referee, the match ends and the game is over. This page represents the ultimate objective of submission grappling: forcing your opponent to concede defeat.

## What Does "Game Over" Mean in BJJ?

**Game Over** is the moment a BJJ match ends through submission. It is the terminal state of the [[game-over]] node in the BJJ Graph knowledge graph — every path through [[Submissions]] and submission-finish [[Transitions]] leads here.

A match ends by submission when:

1. The losing competitor **taps** — strikes the mat, their opponent, or their own body 2–3 times
2. The losing competitor **verbally submits** — says "tap," "stop," or "I quit"
3. The losing competitor **goes limp** — unconsciousness from a blood choke
4. The referee **stops the match** — when a competitor cannot intelligently defend

Once Game Over is reached, there is no recovery, no escape, no scramble. The match is finished.

## How Does a BJJ Match End by Submission?

Submissions work by creating an intolerable physiological condition that forces a tap. Every submission targets one of four mechanisms:

### Blood Chokes (Strangleholds)

Blood chokes compress the carotid arteries, cutting off blood flow to the brain. Unconsciousness follows in 3–8 seconds without a tap.

- **[[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]]** — the most common finish in BJJ and MMA, applied from [[Positions/Back Control]]
- **[[Submissions/Triangle Choke]]** — figure-four leg choke from guard, attacking both carotid arteries
- **[[Submissions/Bow and Arrow Choke]]** — gi-specific choke from back control using collar grip
- **[[Submissions/Anaconda Choke]]** — arm-in choke from front headlock position
- **[[Submissions/Darce Choke]]** — arm-in choke applied from top position
- **[[Submissions/Guillotine Choke]]** — front headlock choke with multiple finish variations
- **[[Submissions/North-South Choke]]** — applied from the [[Positions/North-South]] position
- **[[Submissions/Ezekiel Choke]]** — sleeve-assisted choke, effective from [[Positions/Mount]]

### Air Chokes (Wind Chokes)

Air chokes compress the trachea, restricting airflow. They are typically slower but effective when blood choke grip is unavailable.

- **[[Submissions/Cross Collar Choke]]** — classic gi choke from [[Positions/Mount]] or guard
- **[[Submissions/Arm Triangle]]** — uses the opponent's own shoulder to compress the neck
- **[[Submissions/Loop Choke]]** — collar-based choke catching an opponent who postures
- **[[Submissions/Baseball Bat Choke]]** — gi choke using baseball-bat grip on the collar

### Upper Body Joint Locks

Joint locks hyperextend or rotate a joint past its anatomical limit. The tap must come before ligament or bone damage occurs.

- **[[Submissions/Armbar from Mount]]** — elbow hyperextension from dominant top position
- **[[Submissions/Armbar from Guard]]** — elbow hyperextension attacking from bottom
- **[[Submissions/Kimura]]** — shoulder rotation lock targeting the shoulder joint
- **[[Submissions/Americana]]** — shoulder lock (Ude Garami) applied from top positions
- **[[Submissions/Omoplata]]** — shoulder lock using leg leverage from guard
- **[[Submissions/Gogoplata]]** — shin-to-throat / armbar hybrid from Rubber Guard

### Leg Locks

Leg locks attack the knee, ankle, and hip joints. **High-percentage and dangerous** — they often provide little warning pain before serious injury.

- **[[Submissions/Heel Hook]]** — rotational force on the knee; the highest-percentage leg lock finish
- **[[Submissions/Inside Heel Hook]]** — inside rotation variant, extremely dangerous
- **[[Submissions/Toe Hold]]** — ankle/knee torque using a figure-four hand position
- **[[Submissions/Kneebar]]** — knee hyperextension analogous to an armbar
- **[[Submissions/Estima Lock]]** — foot lock targeting the Achilles and ankle complex
- **[[Submissions/Aoki Lock]]** — wrist/shoulder lock combined with leg entanglement
- **[[Submissions/Ankle Lock from X-Guard]]** — straight ankle lock from leg entanglement

### Compression Locks

Compression locks crush muscles or apply nerve pressure. Legal in specific rule sets (IBJJF restricts some at lower belts).

- **[[Submissions/Calf Slicer]]** — figure-four leg configuration compressing the calf
- **[[Submissions/Bicep Slicer]]** — compresses the bicep against the humerus
- **[[Submissions/Banana Split]]** — hip spread submission from inside leg entanglement
- **[[Submissions/Electric Chair Submission]]** — inside heel hook pressure with body stretch

## What Positions Lead to Game Over?

All submission attempts originate from dominant positions in the BJJ Graph. The most submission-productive positions are:

- **[[Positions/Mount]]** — highest submission rate; direct access to chokes and armbars
- **[[Positions/Back Control]]** — premier position for [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]] and choke variants
- **[[Positions/Side Control]]** — platform for Kimuras, Americanas, and chokes
- **[[Positions/Closed Guard]]** — offensive position for triangles, armbars, and omoplatas from bottom

Leg lock submissions originate primarily from leg entanglement positions: [[Positions/Ashi Garami/Saddle]], [[Positions/Ashi Garami]], [[Positions/Ashi Garami/Honey Hole]], and [[Positions/Ashi Garami/Inside Ashi-Garami]].

## What is the Tap-Out Protocol in BJJ?

**Safety Rule: Always tap early.** The purpose of tapping is to signal submission *before* injury occurs. Once an opponent has a properly secured submission, there is no reliable escape.

### How to Tap Out Correctly

1. **Hand tap** — strike the mat or your opponent's body 2–3 times firmly
2. **Foot tap** — tap with your foot when your hands are trapped
3. **Verbal tap** — say "tap," "stop," or "I quit" clearly and loudly
4. **Body tap** — tap your own leg or torso if the mat is out of reach

### When to Tap

- **Chokes:** Tap when you feel the choke tighten and cannot escape — *before* you lose consciousness
- **Joint locks:** Tap at the *first sensation of joint pressure* — ligament damage happens in milliseconds
- **Leg locks:** Tap immediately on any heel hook — the knee can be destroyed before pain registers
- **Any submission:** Tap when you are caught and escape is not available

### Releasing a Submission

The attacker **must release immediately** upon tap. Holding a submission after a tap is a serious safety violation and etiquette breach. In competition, continued holding after a tap results in disqualification.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most dangerous submission in BJJ?

The **[[Submissions/Heel Hook]]** (particularly the inside heel hook) is the most dangerous submission in BJJ. It applies rotational torque to the knee, which has minimal pain receptors — the joint can be seriously damaged before the tap reflex triggers. Heel hooks are restricted to brown/black belt in IBJJF gi events for this reason.

### Can you escape a submission once it is fully locked in?

Rarely. A properly secured submission leaves no effective escape. This is why **positional defense** — not getting caught in the first place — is the correct strategy. Experienced grapplers focus on escaping the *position* (e.g., escaping [[Positions/Back Control]]) rather than waiting for a submission to be applied.

### Is going limp the same as tapping in BJJ?

Yes. Unconsciousness from a blood choke counts as a submission. The referee stops the match and the unconscious competitor loses. Modern BJJ gyms treat any sign of limp body response as an immediate stoppage. Competitors should **always tap before going unconscious**.

### What submissions are illegal in BJJ competition?

Legality varies by rule set and belt level. Common restrictions:
- **IBJJF:** Heel hooks, reaping, knee bars — restricted below brown/black belt; spinal locks without choke illegal at all belts
- **NAGA:** More permissive; heel hooks typically allowed at advanced divisions
- **Submission Wrestling/ADCC:** Most leg locks and compression locks permitted
- Always check the specific ruleset before competing.

### How does Game Over fit in the BJJ Graph?

In the BJJ Graph, **[[game-over]]** is the single terminal node — a sink with no outgoing edges. Every submission technique that ends a match leads here. It is equivalent to checkmate in chess: once reached, the game state cannot change. Browse [[Transitions]] to trace any submission path to this terminal state.

### What is the difference between a tap and a submission hold?

A **submission** is the technique being applied (e.g., Rear Naked Choke, Heel Hook). The **tap** is the signal of surrender. A submission without a tap is an *attempt*. A tap caused by a submission is **Game Over**.
