**Game Over** is the terminal state of the BJJ Graph — the single endpoint that every submission technique leads to. When a practitioner successfully applies a submission their opponent cannot escape, the match ends. No points, no overtime, no recovery. This page explains every way a grappling match reaches this state.

## What is Game Over in BJJ?

**Game Over** signals the definitive end of a grappling exchange. Unlike points that can be overturned or advantages that can be equalized, a submission finish is absolute — the match is over the moment it is achieved.

In the BJJ Graph, **Game Over** functions as the **sink node**: the terminal state that every submission flows into. Techniques like the [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]], [[Submissions/Armbar from Mount]], [[Submissions/Triangle Choke]], [[Submissions/Heel Hook]], and [[Submissions/Guillotine Choke]] all connect directly to this state — representing the moment a match resolves with certainty.

A match reaches Game Over through one of four mechanisms:

1. **Physical tap** — striking the mat, opponent, or any surface at least twice
2. **Verbal tap** — saying "tap," "stop," or any clear spoken submission signal
3. **Referee stoppage** — when the referee determines a competitor cannot safely continue
4. **Unconsciousness** — when a choke renders a competitor unable to defend

## How Does a BJJ Match End? Submission, Points, and Stoppage Rules

A BJJ match can end in four distinct ways:

1. **Submission (Game Over)** — One competitor applies a technique the opponent cannot escape and must submit to. This is the most decisive finish and the explicit objective of submission grappling.
2. **Points decision** — After time expires with no submission, the competitor with more points wins. Key scoring positions: guard pass (3 pts), takedown (2 pts), mount and [[Positions/Back Control]] (4 pts each).
3. **Advantage count and referee decision** — IBJJF uses advantages as a tiebreaker, then referee decision based on perceived dominance.
4. **Disqualification** — Illegal techniques, unsportsmanlike conduct, or repeated rule violations result in disqualification — technically also a terminal state, but not a submission win.

Submission is universally considered the most decisive finish. The BJJ Graph models the complete path from opening position to Game Over — every technique chains together to create routes to this terminal state.

## What Does Tapping Out Mean in BJJ? Tap Rules and Etiquette

**Tapping out** is the act of physically signaling submission — acknowledging that you cannot escape the technique being applied and choosing safety over continuing. The tap triggers immediate match stoppage.

**How to tap correctly:**
- Strike the mat, your opponent's body, or any surface **at least twice**
- Tap loudly enough for the referee and opponent to notice
- Use your foot if your hands are completely trapped
- Say "tap" or "stop" if physical tapping is impossible

**Why tapping is respected in BJJ:**
- Protects both practitioners from injury during training and competition
- Allows both competitors to continue training the following day
- Demonstrates technical integrity — acknowledging the submission cannot be escaped
- Enables safe study of high-risk techniques like the [[Submissions/Heel Hook]] and [[Submissions/Kimura]]

"Tap early, tap often" is widely taught across all levels of BJJ, especially to beginners. Refusing to tap when a technique is clearly locked risks permanent damage to joints, ligaments, blood vessels, and the cervical spine.

## What is a Verbal Tap in BJJ? When Saying 'Tap' Ends the Match

A **verbal tap** is a spoken submission signal — saying "tap," "stop," "I quit," or any unambiguous verbal declaration. It carries the same legal weight as a physical tap under IBJJF, ADCC, NAGA, and virtually all major rulesets.

**When verbal taps are critical:**
- During a tight [[Submissions/Armbar from Mount]] when both arms are controlled and cannot reach the mat
- Under a [[Submissions/Guillotine Choke]] when head and neck movement is severely restricted
- When pinned under a heavy [[Submissions/Kimura from Side Control]] with no hand access to the mat
- During any technique that immobilizes all limbs simultaneously

Referees actively listen for verbal submissions and will stop the match immediately upon hearing a clear signal. Competitors at all levels should train to vocalize a tap in situations where physical tapping may be insufficient or inaudible.

## What Happens When You Go Unconscious from a Choke in BJJ?

When a competitor loses consciousness from a **blood choke** — a technique that restricts blood flow to the brain via the carotid arteries — the match is stopped immediately by the referee and awarded to the attacker as a submission win.

**The sequence of events:**

1. The choke compresses the carotid arteries, cutting oxygenated blood flow to the brain
2. The competitor loses consciousness, typically within 4–8 seconds of a tight choke
3. The body goes limp — this is the referee's signal to intervene
4. The referee calls "stop" and the attacker releases the choke
5. Medical staff assess the unconscious competitor
6. Consciousness returns within seconds in most cases as blood flow resumes

The most common submissions that cause unconsciousness are blood chokes: the [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]], [[Submissions/Bow and Arrow Choke]], [[Submissions/Triangle Choke]], and [[Submissions/Guillotine Choke]]. Air chokes (windpipe compression) cause unconsciousness more slowly and painfully — most practitioners tap well before losing consciousness to an air choke.

This is why **Game Over** includes referee stoppages: the match has reached its terminal state regardless of whether the competitor consciously submitted.

## Can You Be Forced to Tap? Referee Stoppages Without a Tap

A match can reach **Game Over** without a tap in several scenarios. Referees have full authority to stop a match when:

- A competitor **goes unconscious** — the body going limp ends the match automatically
- A **joint lock is clearly locked** with no response from the defender, indicating inability to tap
- A competitor **cannot intelligently defend** — appearing dazed, disoriented, or unresponsive
- A **corner throws in the towel** — valid in some rulesets where coaches can stop the match
- A **medical emergency** occurs that prevents safe continuation

Refusing to tap to a clearly locked submission is extremely dangerous. Joint locks like the [[Submissions/Heel Hook]] can cause catastrophic ligament damage in fractions of a second. The referee's responsibility is competitor safety — they will intervene before injury occurs when possible.

## Is Tapping Considered Losing in BJJ?

**Technically yes** — the competitor who taps loses that match. However, BJJ culture treats tapping very differently from how "quitting" is viewed in other combat sports.

In BJJ, tapping is understood as:

- **Intelligence over ego** — recognizing a position is mechanically unescapable
- **Respect for training partners** — allowing them to complete their technique safely and learn from it
- **Long-term investment** — protecting your body so you can train the next day, next week, next year
- **Honesty** — acknowledging the technical reality of the exchange without resistance born from ego

The community widely teaches "tap early, tap often" because refusing to tap risks injuries that remove practitioners from training for weeks, months, or permanently. A submission win is the highest form of victory; accepting a submission is equally a mark of a mature, intelligent practitioner.

## What Submissions Lead to Game Over?

Every submission in BJJ leads to **Game Over**. The most common submission finishes in competition are:

**Chokes (blood and air):**
- [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]] — most common finish across all grappling formats
- [[Submissions/Triangle Choke]] — high-percentage from guard positions
- [[Submissions/Guillotine Choke]] — common from front headlock and closed guard
- [[Submissions/Bow and Arrow Choke]] — premier gi submission from [[Positions/Back Control]]
- [[Submissions/Darce Choke]] — common from front headlock and turtle

**Joint locks:**

## What Submissions Are Illegal in BJJ Competition?

Submission legality varies significantly by ruleset and division:

**IBJJF (gi and no-gi, belt-restricted):**
- **Heel hooks** — illegal at white through purple belt; legal at brown and black belt in no-gi
- **Knee reaping** — illegal at all levels
- **Spinal locks without a simultaneous choke** — illegal at all levels
- **Neck cranks** — illegal at all levels
- **Slamming** — illegal at all levels

**ADCC (no-gi submission wrestling):**
- Heel hooks and most leg locks legal for all competitors
- Generally the most permissive major international ruleset
- Slamming remains illegal

**Submission-only formats (EBI, many no-gi promotions):**
- Typically the most permissive — heel hooks often allowed with belt or experience restrictions
- Rule details vary by promotion

Applying an illegal technique typically results in **disqualification** of the attacker — a form of Game Over that does not reflect technical submission superiority.

## What Happens When Time Expires Without a Submission?

When no submission occurs before time expires, the match does not reach **Game Over** through a finish. Instead:

- **IBJJF** — Points decide the winner; advantages serve as tiebreaker, then referee decision
- **ADCC** — Points period followed by overtime rounds; penalties phase into points phase
- **Submission-only (EBI)** — Overtime rounds from specific starting positions; no decisions, forcing a finish
- **Many no-gi promotions** — Overtime structures designed to prevent point decisions and force action toward submission

These formats acknowledge that **Game Over through submission is the ideal outcome** — competition designers increasingly build overtime structures that push toward the terminal submission state rather than point decisions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does 'game over' mean in BJJ?

**Game Over** is the terminal state in submission grappling — the moment a match ends because one competitor taps out, verbally submits, goes unconscious, or the referee stops the contest. In the BJJ Graph, it is the single terminal node that every submission technique connects to. No further grappling occurs once this state is reached.

### What happens when you tap out in BJJ?

When a competitor taps, the match stops immediately. The opponent releases the submission hold, the referee signals the end, and the tapping competitor loses. Tapping is the safest and most respected way to acknowledge a submission — it protects both competitors and enables the continued study of dangerous techniques.

### What counts as a tap in BJJ — do you have to tap the mat?

No. You can tap your opponent's body, your own body, the mat, a post, or any surface. You can tap with your foot if your hands are trapped. A verbal "tap" or "stop" is equally valid. Referees watch for all forms of submission signal.

### What is a verbal tap in BJJ, and is it valid?

A verbal tap — saying "tap," "stop," or a similar signal — is fully valid under all major rulesets including IBJJF, ADCC, and NAGA. It is especially important when your hands are trapped and you cannot physically strike a surface. Train to verbalize your tap in addition to physical tapping.

### What happens if you go unconscious from a choke in BJJ?

The referee stops the match and awards the win to the attacking competitor. Medical staff assess the unconscious practitioner. Most people regain full consciousness within seconds once the choke is released, as blood flow to the brain resumes. This is recorded as a submission win for the attacker.

### What is the difference between a tap-out and a submission in BJJ?

A **submission** is the technique — the [[Submissions/Rear Naked Choke]], [[Submissions/Armbar from Mount]], the [[Submissions/Triangle Choke]]. A **tap-out** is the act of signaling surrender in response to it. You submit to the submission by tapping. In competition records, this is logged as "won by submission" whether the finish was a physical tap, verbal tap, or referee stoppage.

### Why do BJJ matches sometimes end without a tap — what is referee stoppage?

Referees can stop a match without a tap when a competitor is unconscious, when a joint lock is completely locked with no defensive response, when a competitor appears unable to intelligently defend, or when a corner throws in the towel. The referee's primary duty is protecting competitors — they will intervene before serious injury occurs when the competitor cannot or will not tap themselves.
