<section id="overview" class="content-section">

The Achilles Lock is the compression finish of the straight-ankle family, and the inside ashi garami entanglement is where the corpus declares it. Where the Straight Ankle Lock hyperextends the ankle joint, the Achilles Lock drives the blade of the forearm or the wrist bone across the Achilles tendon itself and compresses it against the heel — the disambiguation the corpus draws between the two is a mechanism difference, not a naming preference, and the finishing details follow from it.

From inside ashi garami the attacker has the opponent's leg threaded between their own with the foot trapped high against the ribs or armpit. The finishing sequence is a chain of small commitments: the foot must be captured above the level of your own hip line, the blade must sit on the tendon rather than the soft calf, your own knees must close to remove the escape lane, and only then do the hips extend. Extending before the blade is placed converts a submission into a leg-strength contest that a bigger opponent wins.

The Achilles Lock is legal at every IBJJF belt level in both gi and no-gi, which is why its attempt and success profile does not carry the ruleset asymmetry that the heel hooks at this same seat do. That legality also makes it the first serious leg attack most practitioners meet, and the one whose defensive recognition matters earliest. Failing the finish usually leaves the entanglement intact — you return to ashi garami and can rebuild — while losing the leg entirely concedes the position and puts you inside the opponent's guard.

## Playing as Attacker

[[Submissions/Achilles Lock/from Inside Ashi-Garami/Attacker|→ Full Attacker Guide]]

### Key Principles


- Placement before power — the blade sits on the tendon before any hip extension begins

- Capture the foot ABOVE your own hip line; below it, extension shortens the lever instead of loading it

- Close your knees to remove the escape lane before you commit to the finish

- Extend through the hips and shoulders together, not by pulling with the arms

- Compression, not rotation — this is the straight-ankle mechanism, and rotating turns it into a different and more dangerous attack

- Ramp slowly on every repetition; the tendon fails faster than a partner can tap

### Common Mistakes


- **Extending before the blade is seated**
  - Consequence: The pressure lands on the calf, the opponent is warned, and the attack becomes a leg-strength contest a heavier opponent wins.
  - Correction: Confirm the radial edge is on the tendon and only then begin the extension — placement always precedes power.

- **Letting the captured foot slip below your hip line**
  - Consequence: Extension no longer loads the ankle, and the opponent extracts the leg during the arch.
  - Correction: Re-capture the foot high against the ribs or armpit before attempting the finish again.

- **Leaving the knees open**
  - Consequence: The opponent rotates the knee and turns the heel out of the blade, escaping or reversing into their own entanglement.
  - Correction: Pinch the knees across their thigh as a distinct step before the arm ever moves.

<section id="defender-summary" class="content-section">

## Playing as Defender

[[Submissions/Achilles Lock/from Inside Ashi-Garami/Defender|→ Full Defender Guide]]

### Key Principles


- Act on the capture, not on the pain — by the time it hurts the tap window is nearly closed

- Rotating the knee inward turns the heel away from the blade and is the highest-percentage answer

- Keep your heel low and your foot below their hip line; the capture height is what makes their extension work

- Your free leg is your best tool — it boots their hip, blocks the roll, and creates the space to extract

- Sit up early: a defender lying flat has no leverage and no access to their own trapped foot

- Tap early and without ego on this one — a ruptured Achilles is measured in months, not weeks


### Recognition Cues


- Your foot is being drawn upward toward their ribs or armpit rather than staying at their belt line

- Their knees pinch together across your thigh, which removes your rotation escape

- Their arm threads under your heel and the forearm turns edge-on against the back of your ankle

- Their shoulders begin travelling back over their hips — the extension is starting and the window is closing


### Escape Paths


- Knee rotation — turn the knee inward so the heel leaves the blade, then extract the leg over the top of their thigh

- Boot-and-roll — free foot to their hip, roll toward the trapped side, and unwind the entanglement as you come up

- Sit-up hand fight — post up, strip the finishing arm before the figure four closes, and back out of the entanglement

<section id="variations" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Variations


**Blade-across finish**: The standard mechanic: the blade of the radius sits directly across the Achilles tendon, the wrist is cupped under the heel, and the finish comes from hip extension with the shoulders driving back. (When to use: The default whenever the foot is captured cleanly above your hip line.)


**Wrist-bone compression**: Instead of the flat forearm, the styloid process of the wrist is driven into the tendon, concentrating the pressure over a much smaller area. It finishes faster and with far less extension, which also makes it considerably more dangerous. (When to use: Against a thick or flexible ankle where the flat blade cannot generate enough compression — and only with a trusted partner.)


**Knee-line hip switch**: Rather than extending straight back, switch the hips toward the opponent's knee line while keeping the blade fixed. The angle change loads the tendon without requiring a long extension, which is useful when your back has nowhere to go. (When to use: When the opponent has posted or is following you and a straight extension gives them a base to follow with.)

<section id="safety-considerations" class="content-section">

## Safety Guide

**Injury Risks**:

| Injury | Severity | Recovery Time |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| Achilles tendon rupture or partial tear | CRITICAL | 6-12 months, commonly surgical, with a risk of permanent loss of push-off strength |
| Ankle ligament damage (ATFL and deltoid ligament) | High | 4-8 weeks with rehabilitation; longer if the joint was loaded explosively |
| Calf muscle strain or tear from a misplaced blade | Medium | 2-6 weeks depending on severity |
| Plantar fascia strain | Low | 1-3 weeks with rest |


**Application Speed**: SLOW and progressive — a minimum of 3-5 seconds from first pressure to full extension. The Achilles tendon gives very little warning before it fails, so the tap window is short and the ramp must be long. Never spike, jerk, or arch explosively.

**Tap Signals**:
- Verbal tap — the word 'tap' or any distress vocalisation
- Physical hand tap on the partner, the mat, or their own body
- Foot tap with the free leg, which is often the only limb they can reach with
- Sudden total loss of resistance or going limp, which must be treated as a tap


**Release Protocol**:
1. Release the hip extension FIRST — the compression comes off before the grip does
2. Open the figure four or the grip and let the ankle return to neutral without pulling on it
3. Open the knees and free the trapped leg completely before disengaging your own hips
4. Check in verbally with your partner and watch them bear weight on the leg before the next round


**Training Restrictions**:
- Drill the blade placement and the finishing angle statically before any live application
- No explosive or 'catch' applications in live rolling — the tendon fails faster than a partner can tap
- Beginners should establish the position and stop there, applying the finish only under direct supervision
- Do not combine the finish with a simultaneous sweep or scramble; a moving opponent cannot tap reliably
