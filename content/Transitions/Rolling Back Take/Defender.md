<section id="overview" class="content-section">

Defending the Rolling Back Take requires early recognition and immediate defensive action from the turtle bottom position. The technique is dangerous precisely because it bypasses the incremental controls that give defenders time to react. Your primary defensive objective is to prevent the roll from initiating or, if it begins, to disrupt the rotation before your opponent can land with control. Understanding the attacker's weight loading patterns and grip sequences allows you to recognize the setup phase and deploy countermeasures before momentum becomes irrecoverable. Successful defense relies on maintaining structural integrity in your turtle, controlling at least one of the attacker's grips, and being prepared to transition to guard recovery or scramble positions when the roll is disrupted.

**Opponent's Starting Position**: [[Positions/Turtle]] (Top)

</section>

<section id="recognition-cues" class="content-section">

## How to Recognize This Attack

<p class="section-subtitle">How do you know when someone is attempting Rolling Back Take?</p>

- Attacker's chest pressure shifts from centered on your back to loading heavily onto one shoulder, indicating the rolling direction
- Attacker secures an over-under grip configuration with one arm threading under your far armpit and the other wrapping over your near shoulder or head
- Attacker's head drops tight against your shoulder blade with chin tucked, positioning for the forward roll
- You feel the attacker's hips lift slightly off the mat as they load forward momentum onto your upper back
- Attacker's far-side arm pulls your far shoulder toward them while their chest drives forward over your shoulder line


</section>

<section id="key-principles" class="content-section">

## Key Defensive Principles

<p class="section-subtitle">What are the key principles for defending Rolling Back Take?</p>

- Recognize weight loading on your upper back as the primary pre-attack cue and react before the roll initiates
- Maintain active hands that strip grips rather than staying in passive defensive turtle shell
- Post the far arm wide when you feel the attacker shifting weight toward one shoulder to block the rolling path
- Keep your elbows tight to your knees to prevent underhook penetration that enables the roll
- Use lateral movement to disrupt the attacker's alignment and prevent them from loading weight evenly
- Turn into the attacker during the roll rather than allowing them to complete the full rotation behind you


</section>

<section id="defensive-options" class="content-section">

## Defensive Options

<p class="section-subtitle">What can you do to defend against Rolling Back Take?</p>

### 1. Post far arm wide and base out to block the rolling path

- **When to use**: As soon as you feel the attacker loading weight onto one shoulder and before they initiate forward momentum
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Turtle]]
- **If successful**: Attacker's roll is blocked and they must abandon the technique, returning to standard turtle top with you maintaining defensive turtle
- **Risk**: Extended arm may be vulnerable to armbar or crucifix if attacker transitions to arm attacks

### 2. Turn into the attacker during the roll to recover guard

- **When to use**: During the rotation phase when the attacker has committed to the roll but has not yet landed with hooks
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Turtle]]
- **If successful**: You face the attacker and recover to closed guard or half guard, neutralizing the back take entirely
- **Risk**: If timing is off, you may end up in a worse position with the attacker on top in mount or side control

### 3. Strip the under-arm grip and drive forward to flatten attacker

- **When to use**: During the setup phase when you feel the attacker threading their arm under your far armpit before they load weight
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Turtle]]
- **If successful**: Without the under-arm grip, the attacker cannot maintain connection during the roll and loses the technique entirely
- **Risk**: Focusing on grip stripping may create openings for alternative attacks like front headlock or traditional back take

### 4. Sprawl hips back and lower base to prevent weight loading

- **When to use**: When you feel the attacker shifting their weight forward onto your shoulders but before the roll initiates
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Turtle]]
- **If successful**: Lowered base prevents the attacker from generating sufficient momentum to complete the roll over your shoulder
- **Risk**: Extended sprawl may create space that allows attacker to switch to traditional hook insertion or front headlock



</section>

<section id="favorable-outcomes" class="content-section">

## Best-Case Outcomes for Defender

<p class="section-subtitle">What is the best outcome when defending Rolling Back Take?</p>

### → [[Positions/Turtle]]

Block the roll by posting your far arm wide and basing out when you feel the attacker loading weight onto your shoulder. Strip their under-arm grip with your near hand while maintaining structural integrity in your turtle. The attacker returns to standard top turtle without having advanced position.

### → [[Positions/Turtle]]

Time your turn to coincide with the attacker's committed rotation. As they roll over your shoulder, turn your hips and shoulders toward them aggressively, using their own momentum to help you face them. If executed correctly during the mid-roll phase, you recover to a guard position or at minimum force a scramble where you end up facing your opponent.



</section>

<section id="common-mistakes" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Common Defensive Mistakes

<p class="section-subtitle">What mistakes should you avoid when defending Rolling Back Take?</p>

### 1. Remaining completely static in turtle without reacting to the weight shift

- **Consequence**: Attacker loads weight freely and executes the roll with full momentum, landing directly on your back with hooks already threatening
- **Correction**: Maintain active defensive posture with constant micro-movements. As soon as you feel weight loading onto one shoulder, immediately post the far arm, strip grips, or shift your base laterally to disrupt alignment

### 2. Attempting to stand up as the attacker initiates the roll

- **Consequence**: Your upward momentum is redirected by the attacker's forward roll, pulling you backward and often resulting in an even more dominant back control position for the attacker
- **Correction**: When the roll is already initiating, do not stand. Instead, turn into the attacker or flatten your base to stall the rotation. Standing attempts should only occur before the attacker has loaded their weight

### 3. Extending both arms to post instead of keeping one arm defending grips

- **Consequence**: Both arms posted leaves your upper body completely undefended, giving the attacker free access to establish the over-under grip needed for the roll and potentially exposing both arms to crucifix
- **Correction**: Post only the far arm while keeping the near arm active for grip fighting. The near hand should be stripping the attacker's under-arm grip or defending your neck while the far arm blocks the rolling path

### 4. Turning away from the attacker instead of into them during the roll

- **Consequence**: Turning away exposes your back further and accelerates the attacker's rotation, often resulting in them achieving back control more easily than if you had stayed still
- **Correction**: Always turn toward the attacker during the roll, not away. Turning into them disrupts the rotation angle and puts you in position to recover guard. Your goal is to face them, not to run from the roll

### 5. Panicking and flattening to the mat when feeling the roll initiate

- **Consequence**: Flattening eliminates your base entirely and makes it trivial for the attacker to complete the roll and establish hooks on a prone opponent with no defensive structure
- **Correction**: Maintain your four-point turtle base or actively turn into the attacker. Flattening should never be your response. If you feel the roll starting, either post aggressively or turn with purpose toward the attacker



</section>

<section id="training-progressions" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Training Progressions

<p class="section-subtitle">How do you train defense against Rolling Back Take?</p>

**Week 1-2: Recognition Drill** - Identifying rolling back take setup cues from turtle bottom
Partner telegraphs the rolling back take setup at slow speed, loading weight and establishing grips. Bottom player calls out each recognition cue as they feel it: weight shift, grip threading, head positioning. No actual roll execution. Build the sensory vocabulary for recognizing the attack before it happens.

**Week 3-4: Primary Defense Mechanics** - Posting, grip stripping, and base widening responses
Partner sets up the rolling back take at moderate speed. Bottom player practices primary defenses: far arm post, grip stripping with near hand, and lateral base shifts. Partner completes the roll only if defense is not deployed in time. Focus on reaction speed and proper defensive structure.

**Week 5-8: Mid-Roll Recovery** - Turning into the attacker during committed rolls
Partner executes the roll at realistic speed. Bottom player practices turning into the attacker during the rotation phase, recovering to guard or scramble positions. Work on timing the turn correctly and maintaining arm control during the scramble. Progressively increase partner's commitment and speed.

**Week 9+: Full Resistance Integration** - Combining all defensive layers against live attacks
Positional sparring from turtle bottom against partner using full-speed rolling back take attempts mixed with traditional back takes. Bottom player must read which attack is coming and deploy the appropriate defense. Track success rates and identify which defensive layer needs the most work.
