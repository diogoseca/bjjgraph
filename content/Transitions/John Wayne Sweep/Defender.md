<section id="overview" class="content-section">

Defending the John Wayne Sweep requires understanding its three-phase execution sequence and intercepting at the earliest possible stage. The sweep depends on a chain of prerequisites: two-on-one sleeve control, angular positioning, the pistol grip break, and coordinated hook elevation. Each phase presents a distinct defensive window, and the earlier you intervene, the higher your success rate. Once the rotation begins with full momentum, defensive options narrow dramatically.

From the top position against active butterfly hooks, your primary defensive objective is denying the two-on-one grip from isolating your arm across your centerline while keeping your weight back and off the hooks. If the grip is established, your secondary objective becomes maintaining a strong posting base while working to recover the controlled arm. The sweep becomes nearly unstoppable once both your posting base and arm control are compromised, so defensive urgency escalates with each phase the attacker completes.

Effective defense integrates grip fighting, base management, and tactical weight distribution. Keeping your weight back off the hooks and denying the two-on-one neutralizes the setup. If the attacker achieves the angle, strong posting combined with driving your weight forward and down can nullify the elevation. Skilled defenders also use the attacker's commitment against them by timing guard passes during the rotation attempt, capitalizing on the momentary vulnerability created when the bottom player fully commits to the sweep.

**Opponent's Starting Position**: [[Positions/Butterfly Guard/Butterfly Hook Control]] (Bottom)

<section id="recognition-cues" class="content-section">

## How to Recognize This Attack

<p class="section-subtitle">How do you know when someone is attempting John Wayne Sweep?</p>

- Opponent secures a two-on-one grip on your lead sleeve, pulling your arm across their body toward their opposite hip
- Opponent's body angles approximately 45 degrees to the sleeve-control side while keeping active butterfly hooks inside your thighs
- Opponent's free hand forms a pistol shape and moves toward your posted hand on the mat, signaling the signature grip break
- You feel simultaneous upward hook elevation and lateral pulling on your controlled arm creating a rotational force vector
- Opponent's head drops below your chest level and moves to the outside of your controlled arm as they come up strongly onto their hip


</section>

<section id="key-principles" class="content-section">

## Key Defensive Principles

<p class="section-subtitle">What are the key principles for defending John Wayne Sweep?</p>

- Deny the two-on-one grip by keeping your elbows tight and grip-fighting actively from the top
- Keep your weight back and off the butterfly hooks to deny their elevation leverage
- Maintain at least one strong posting point at all times to resist the rotational sweep
- Keep your elbows tight to your torso to prevent arm isolation across your centerline
- Recognize the pistol grip break motion and immediately redistribute weight to maintain base
- Use the attacker's commitment against them by timing counter-passes during full sweep attempts
- When the hooks elevate, drive your hips forward and down to flatten their angle


</section>

<section id="defensive-options" class="content-section">

## Defensive Options

<p class="section-subtitle">What can you do to defend against John Wayne Sweep?</p>

### 1. Strip the two-on-one grip by circling your controlled elbow back toward your hip and pulling your wrist free with a strong circular motion

- **When to use**: Immediately when you feel the opponent establishing two-on-one control on your sleeve, before they can create an angle. This is the highest-percentage defense because it stops the sweep at its foundation.
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Half Guard]]
- **If successful**: Returns to a neutral top position with the opponent unable to execute the sweep, allowing you to re-establish base and passing grips
- **Risk**: If the grip strip fails, you may overextend your arm and give the opponent a deeper angle for the sweep

### 2. Drop your weight back and off the hooks while widening your base and posting to kill the elevation

- **When to use**: When you feel the opponent creating angular positioning and loading your weight onto the hooks. Best used before the pistol grip break occurs.
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Half Guard]]
- **If successful**: Removes your weight from the hooks and kills the elevation angle, resetting to a dominant top position where you can work your passing game
- **Risk**: Dropping weight back too far can let the opponent disengage and re-pummel for an arm drag to the back

### 3. Drive your hips forward and down into the opponent to flatten their angle and pin the hooks, then counter-pass

- **When to use**: When the opponent has established the angle but committed fully to the rotation. Use their momentary guard opening to pass.
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Half Guard]]
- **If successful**: Flattens the opponent and opens a smash pass or over-under pass while their legs are occupied with elevation rather than retention
- **Risk**: If the opponent still has the two-on-one secured, driving forward can fuel their sweep instead of stopping it

### 4. Post the free hand wide with a slightly bent arm and redistribute weight over the post when you feel the pistol break attempt

- **When to use**: When the opponent attempts the signature pistol grip break on your posted hand. Stiffen the post slightly, widen your base, and shift your center of gravity over it.
- **Targets**: [[Positions/Half Guard]]
- **If successful**: Maintains your base structure despite the grip break attempt, forcing the opponent to abandon the sweep or transition to a different attack
- **Risk**: Posting with a straight arm creates vulnerability to a kimura attack if the opponent switches from grip break to submission



</section>

## Best-Case Outcomes for Defender

<p class="section-subtitle">What is the best outcome when defending John Wayne Sweep?</p>

### → [[Positions/Half Guard]]

Strip the two-on-one grip early by circling your elbow back to your hip, then re-establish base and passing posture. Alternatively, drop your weight back off the hooks to nullify the elevation and settle into a half guard pass.

### → [[Positions/Half Guard]]

Time a counter-pass during the opponent's sweep commitment. When they fully commit to the rotation, their butterfly structure is momentarily weakened. Drive forward through their legs with a smash pass or over-under pass, flattening their guard and advancing toward side control.

<section id="common-mistakes" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Common Defensive Mistakes

<p class="section-subtitle">What mistakes should you avoid when defending John Wayne Sweep?</p>

### 1. Allowing the two-on-one grip to establish without immediately contesting it through active grip fighting

- **Consequence**: Once the two-on-one is secured, your arm is isolated across the opponent's centerline, removing one posting option and making the sweep significantly easier to execute
- **Correction**: Fight grips immediately and continuously. The moment you feel the opponent grabbing your sleeve with both hands, circle your elbow back toward your hip and pull your wrist free. Prevent the grip from settling by keeping your arms active.

### 2. Leaning forward and loading your weight onto the hooks when you feel off-balanced by the grip pull

- **Consequence**: Your forward momentum becomes the primary energy source for the sweep, making elevation effortless for the attacker and the rotation nearly unstoppable
- **Correction**: When you feel the pull from the two-on-one grip, resist the instinct to drive your weight onto the hooks. Instead, sit your weight back, widen your base, and work to recover the controlled arm from a balanced posture.

### 3. Posting with a straight, locked-out arm when the opponent attempts the pistol grip break

- **Consequence**: Creates a rigid lever that the pistol motion can easily break, and exposes the arm to a kimura attack if the opponent switches strategy
- **Correction**: Post with a slightly bent arm and distribute weight broadly rather than concentrating it on a single point. Keep your elbow slightly bent and your hand wide, ready to adjust the post dynamically.

### 4. Turning away from the sweep direction during rotation instead of facing into it

- **Consequence**: Exposes your back to a potential back take if the sweep partially succeeds, converting a recovery situation into an even worse positional crisis
- **Correction**: Always face toward the direction of the sweep rotation. If you feel the rotation beginning, turn into it and work to establish or maintain the top position.

### 5. Attempting to stand up while the two-on-one grip is still fully secured on your arm

- **Consequence**: Standing with a compromised arm gives the opponent a direct entry to X-guard or single leg X-guard, trading one bad position for another
- **Correction**: Strip or at least partially compromise the two-on-one grip before standing. If you must stand with the grip intact, immediately circle the controlled arm free as you create distance.



</section>

<section id="training-progressions" class="content-section hide-minimal">

## Training Progressions

<p class="section-subtitle">How do you train defense against John Wayne Sweep?</p>

**Phase 1: Recognition and Grip Prevention** - Identifying John Wayne Sweep setups and preventing the two-on-one grip
Partner slowly works through the John Wayne Sweep setup phases while you practice recognizing each stage. Focus exclusively on stripping the two-on-one grip using elbow circles and wrist extraction. No passing or countering yet - just recognition and grip prevention at various stages of the setup.

**Phase 2: Base Under Sweep Pressure** - Maintaining posting structure during active sweep attempts
Partner establishes the full John Wayne Sweep setup including the two-on-one grip and angle. Practice maintaining base through strong posting, weight distribution, and keeping your weight off the hooks as the partner attempts the full sweep with increasing intensity. Focus on staying balanced through the rotation attempt rather than stripping grips.

**Phase 3: Counter-Passing and Tactical Weight Movement** - Converting defensive survival into offensive passing opportunities
Partner attempts the full John Wayne Sweep against your active defense. Practice keeping your weight off the hooks, posting strongly, and timing counter-passes when the sweep attempt fails. Work on reading which defensive response is appropriate based on the stage of the sweep setup.

**Phase 4: Live Defensive Sparring** - Defending against the John Wayne Sweep under full resistance
Positional sparring starting against butterfly hooks with the partner specifically hunting for the John Wayne Sweep. Defend with your full technical repertoire including grip fighting, weight management, posting, and counter-passing. Partner chains the sweep with other butterfly attacks to simulate realistic offensive pressure and test defensive adaptability.
